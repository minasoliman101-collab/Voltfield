Identifying an electrical part from what is printed on it

The situation is nearly always the same: there is a device in front of you, it has some markings, and you need to know what it is before you can order another one. Sometimes the nameplate is complete. More often it is a partial catalogue number, a faded label, or a stamping that means something only to the manufacturer who applied it.

This tool works from the markings you can actually read. Partial part numbers, manufacturer logos, frame stampings and rating plate fragments are all usable inputs — the aim is to narrow to a family and a likely configuration, which is generally enough to have a productive conversation with a manufacturer.

What the markings usually mean

Most industrial electrical part numbers are structured rather than sequential: segments of the number encode frame size, rating, trip type, voltage class and accessories. That structure is why a partial number is still useful — the leading characters typically identify the family, and the family is what determines where to look for everything else.

Where to find the markings

  • The rating plate carries the electrical characteristics: voltage, current, interrupting rating, frequency, and the standards the device is listed to. This is the authoritative source when it is legible.
  • The catalogue number is usually separate from the rating plate, often moulded or stamped into the case rather than printed, which is why it frequently survives when the label does not.
  • A date code narrows the platform generation, which matters when a family ran for decades across several revisions.
  • Case moulding and terminal arrangement identify a family on their own when nothing legible remains. Frame proportions and lug configuration are genuinely diagnostic.
Photograph the device before removing it. Markings are routinely destroyed during removal, and the orientation of a device in its enclosure — which way the line side faces, how the accessories are wired — is information you will want later and cannot recover afterwards. A photograph of the installation costs nothing and answers questions the part number does not.

When identification is not enough

Identifying the part tells you what is installed. It does not tell you that the same part is still the right choice. If the installation has changed — more available fault current after a service upgrade, a different load profile, a revised code requirement — then like-for-like replacement can reproduce a problem rather than solve one. Identification is the first step, not the whole answer.

Getting a usable result

  • Give the longest fragment you can read, not the cleanest. Six uncertain characters narrow the search far more than three certain ones.
  • Include the manufacturer even when the number is incomplete. Numbering conventions are manufacturer-specific, so knowing the maker often resolves an otherwise ambiguous fragment.
  • Note the physical size. Frame dimensions distinguish families that share a numbering prefix, and they are measurable when nothing is legible.
  • Record the application, not just the device. Voltage, phase and what it feeds are what let someone confirm the identification is plausible rather than merely consistent.

Common questions

Can a part be identified from a photograph alone?

Sometimes, to family level. Case geometry, terminal arrangement and moulded markings are distinctive enough that an experienced eye can place many devices without a legible label. What a photograph generally cannot give you is the configuration — trip rating, accessories, interrupting rating — because those vary within a family that looks identical from outside.

What if the nameplate is completely illegible?

Work from the physical evidence and the context. Frame dimensions, mounting arrangement, terminal type and the conductor sizes landing on it bound the rating considerably. The panel schedule, the original drawings, or the devices either side of it in a lineup often resolve the rest. This is slower than reading a label, but it is rarely a dead end.

Is the catalogue number the same as the part number?

Usually, but not always, and the distinction causes real confusion. Some manufacturers separate a catalogue number used for ordering from an internal part or model number stamped on the device, and resellers may add their own SKU on top. When they disagree, the number the manufacturer uses for ordering is the one that gets you the right part.

Does the date code matter?

It matters more than people expect. Long-running product families are revised repeatedly, and a revision can change accessory compatibility or the trip unit interface without changing the catalogue number. When ordering an accessory for an older device, the date code is often what determines whether it will fit.
