dc|Transformer Components|transformer-components|Transformer components and spare parts|The parts a transformer is built from and rebuilt with: bushings, tap changers, radiators, insulating fluid, core steel, winding conductor and the instrumentation that watches all of it.|A transformer is not a single long-lead item so much as an assembly of them. Core steel and bushings have their own supply constraints, and a unit waiting on one component is just as late as a unit waiting on the whole transformer. These are also the parts that decide whether an ageing unit can be refurbished in place or has to be replaced, which is a very different budget and a very different schedule.|Which transformer parts drive lead time, and what can be rebuilt rather than replaced?

dc|Switchgear Components|switchgear-components|Switchgear components and repair parts|Instrument transformers, bus insulators, trip units, contact and arc-chute kits, enclosure hardware and the control wiring that ties a lineup together.|Most switchgear spending after commissioning is component spending. Breakers are rebuilt with contact kits and arc chutes, trip units are swapped to change protection behaviour, and CTs and VTs are replaced when a metering or protection scheme changes. For an obsolete frame these parts often decide whether an existing lineup can be maintained at all, or whether the whole assembly has to be replaced to keep a facility protected.|What keeps existing switchgear serviceable, and which parts change its protection behaviour?

dc|Genset Components|genset-components|Generator set components|Engines, alternators, controls and governors, cooling and exhaust, and the fuel and starting systems that decide whether a standby set actually starts.|A generator set is an assembly with three independent failure domains: the prime mover, the electrical machine, and everything that has to work before either of them does. Standby installations fail far more often on fuel condition, batteries and controls than on the engine itself, because those are the parts that sit idle between exercises. Emissions tier on the engine governs where a set may be installed and how many hours it may run, which is frequently a stricter constraint than its power rating.|What actually fails on a standby generator, and what governs where one can be installed?

dc|Grounding & Bonding|grounding-bonding|Grounding, bonding and surge protection|Ground bars, exothermic connections, lugs and earthing kits, plus the surge protective devices that clamp transients before they reach equipment.|Grounding and bonding do two different jobs and are routinely conflated. Grounding establishes a reference to earth; bonding ties metal parts together so fault current has a low-impedance path back to its source and a protective device actually operates. The equipment grounding conductor is not the neutral, and treating it as one is a recurring and dangerous error. Surge protection is a separate layer again, rated by where it sits relative to the main overcurrent device.|What is the difference between grounding and bonding, and where does surge protection fit?

dc|Monitoring & Controls|monitoring-controls|Power monitoring and protective relays|Revenue and power-quality meters feeding building management and DCIM systems, and the protective relays that decide when a breaker opens.|These two categories look similar and answer to very different requirements. Metering accuracy class governs what a reading may be used for -- revenue settlement demands far more than trend monitoring, and it is a commercial obligation rather than an engineering preference. Protective relays are not device-level settings at all: a relay is configured as part of a coordinated scheme, so changing one setting changes what happens everywhere downstream of it.|What accuracy does metering need, and why is a relay setting never a local decision?
