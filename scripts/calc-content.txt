===voltage-drop|voltage-drop|Voltage Drop Calculator|Voltage Drop Calculator (Cable Sizing)|Calculate voltage drop on a feeder using the K-factor method, check it against the 3% and 5% design limits, and size the conductor. Free, no signup.
--intro
Voltage drop is the loss along a conductor between the source and the load. It is not a code violation in itself — the NEC states its 3% branch-circuit and 5% total figures as recommendations in informational notes, not requirements — but equipment is rated to operate within a voltage band, and a long run that drops too far will leave a motor starting hard, a contactor chattering, or a drive faulting on undervoltage. On a long feeder it is usually voltage drop, not ampacity, that forces the conductor up a size.
--formula
VD = (2 &times; K &times; I &times; D) / CM  [single-phase]
VD = (1.732 &times; K &times; I &times; D) / CM  [three-phase]
--vars
K :: Resistivity constant: 12.9 for copper, 21.2 for aluminium, in ohm-cmil/ft
I :: Load current in amperes
D :: One-way circuit length in feet
CM :: Conductor cross-section in circular mils
2 or 1.732 :: Accounts for the return path: two conductors single-phase, the square root of three for three-phase
--example
A 100 A three-phase load at 480 V, 150 ft away, on 2 AWG copper (66,360 cmil):
VD = (1.732 &times; 12.9 &times; 100 &times; 150) / 66,360 = 5.05 V
As a percentage: 5.05 / 480 = 1.05%. Comfortably inside 3%.
Move the same load to 400 ft and it becomes 13.5 V, or 2.8% — still inside the limit, but with nothing left for the branch circuit beyond it.
--standard
The K-factor method is the standard hand calculation. NEC 210.19(A) and 215.2(A) informational notes give the 3% branch and 5% total figures. For exact work, IEEE Std 141 (the Red Book) covers the full method with reactance included.
--limits
This is a resistance-only calculation. It ignores conductor reactance, which matters on larger conductors and longer runs — above roughly 250 ft, or above about 4/0, the reactive component starts to dominate and this method under-reads. It also assumes a 75&deg;C conductor temperature and a balanced load.
--mistakes
Using one-way distance where the formula wants it and then doubling it again — the 2 and the 1.732 already account for the return path. Sizing to ampacity first and never checking drop on a long run. Applying the single-phase formula to a three-phase circuit, which over-states drop by about 15%.

===transformer-sizing|transformer-sizing|Transformer Sizing Calculator|Transformer Sizing Calculator (kW to kVA)|Convert a kW load to the kVA rating you need, round to the nearest standard ANSI/IEEE C57.12.00 nameplate, and get full-load amps. Free, no signup.
--intro
Transformers are rated in kVA — apparent power — while loads are usually described in kW, real power. The difference is power factor, and ignoring it is how a transformer ends up undersized on day one. Once you have the kVA figure you cannot simply order it: transformers are built to a standard series of nameplate ratings, so the practical answer is always the next size up.
--formula
kVA = kW / PF
FLA = kVA &times; 1000 / (1.732 &times; V)  [three-phase]
FLA = kVA &times; 1000 / V  [single-phase]
--vars
kW :: Real power drawn by the load
PF :: Power factor, between 0 and 1
V :: Line-to-line secondary voltage
FLA :: Full-load amperes at the nameplate rating
--example
A 400 kW load at 0.85 power factor:
kVA = 400 / 0.85 = 470.6 kVA
The standard series runs 300, 500, 750, 1000 — so the unit specified is 500 kVA.
At 480 V three-phase: FLA = 500 &times; 1000 / (1.732 &times; 480) = 601 A.
--standard
Standard nameplate ratings come from ANSI/IEEE C57.12.00. Loading practice beyond nameplate is covered by IEEE C57.91, which governs how far and how long a unit may be run over rating before insulation life is affected.
--limits
This sizes for steady-state load only. It does not account for future growth, harmonic heating, non-linear load, or motor starting, all of which argue for headroom. It also says nothing about impedance, which is the other half of a transformer specification and the one that sets downstream fault current.
--mistakes
Sizing on kW and never dividing by power factor, which under-sizes by however far PF sits below unity. Sizing exactly to calculated load with no growth allowance. Choosing on kVA alone and leaving %Z to the supplier, which is how switchgear ends up under-rated for the fault current the transformer can deliver.

===fault-current|fault-current|Available Fault Current Calculator|Fault Current Calculator (Short Circuit)|Estimate available symmetrical fault current at a transformer secondary from kVA, voltage and %Z, and check equipment withstand ratings. Free, no signup.
--intro
Available fault current is how much current the system can deliver into a bolted short circuit at a given point. It sets the interrupting rating every protective device downstream must meet, and the withstand rating of the gear that has to survive the event. The counter-intuitive part: a lower transformer impedance means a stiffer source and therefore a higher fault current, so specifying a low-%Z transformer makes the downstream equipment problem worse, not better.
--formula
Isc = (100,000 &times; kVA) / (1.732 &times; V &times; %Z)
--vars
kVA :: Transformer nameplate rating
V :: Line-to-line secondary voltage
%Z :: Transformer impedance as a percentage of rated voltage
Isc :: Symmetrical RMS fault current in amperes
--example
A 1000 kVA transformer at 480 V with 5.75% impedance:
Isc = (100,000 &times; 1000) / (1.732 &times; 480 &times; 5.75) = 20,919 A
So roughly 21 kA at the secondary terminals, and the main breaker needs an interrupting rating above that. Drop the impedance to 4% and the same transformer delivers about 30 kA.
--standard
IEEE Std 141 covers the full method. For arc-flash and coordination work the governing analysis is IEEE 1584. Equipment interrupting ratings are marked per UL 489 for moulded-case breakers and ANSI C37 for power breakers.
--limits
This is an infinite-source estimate: it assumes the utility can supply unlimited current into the transformer primary, which over-states the real figure — conservative, and therefore safe for a first pass. It ignores cable impedance between the transformer and the point of interest, which reduces fault current with distance, and it ignores motor contribution, which increases it for the first few cycles.
--mistakes
Using the calculated value as a final number instead of commissioning a study. Forgetting motor contribution on a motor-heavy bus. Assuming a lower impedance is always better. Applying the secondary-terminal figure to equipment far downstream, where the real value is lower.

===arc-flash|arc-flash|Arc-Flash Incident Energy Calculator|Arc-Flash Incident Energy Calculator|Estimate arc-flash incident energy and boundary with the Lee and Doughty equations, as a first pass before a full IEEE 1584 study. Free, no signup.
--intro
Incident energy is the thermal energy an arc would deliver to a surface at a working distance, in calories per square centimetre. It determines the PPE category and the arc-flash boundary that must appear on the equipment label. Two inputs dominate it, and only one is obvious: available fault current, and how long the upstream device takes to clear. Halving clearing time roughly halves incident energy, which is why protection settings matter more to arc-flash exposure than almost anything else.
--formula
Above 600 V the Lee equation models an open-air arc; at 600 V and below the Doughty equations cover open-air and arc-in-a-box cases. In all of them, incident energy is directly proportional to arcing time, rises with available fault current, and falls steeply with working distance.
--vars
Bolted fault current :: From the fault-current calculation at that point
Clearing time :: How long the upstream protective device takes to interrupt, in seconds
Working distance :: Distance from the arc to the worker's face and chest, commonly 18 inches for LV gear
System voltage :: Above 600 V the calculator uses the Lee equation; at 600 V and below, the Doughty equations. Both are from NFPA 70E Annex D
--example
At 480 V in an enclosure, with 22.6 kA bolted fault current and an 18 in working distance, the Doughty equation gives 4.3 cal/cm&sup2; at 0.1 s clearing and 8.5 cal/cm&sup2; at 0.2 s. Double the time, double the energy: incident energy is directly proportional to arcing time. The arc-flash boundary grows less, from about 3.6 ft to 5.7 ft, because energy falls off steeply with distance. A breaker setting change that shaves 100 ms off clearing can move a task into a lower PPE category without touching the hardware.
--standard
The Lee and Doughty equations are documented in NFPA 70E Annex D. IEEE 1584 is the governing standard for a full arc-flash calculation. NFPA 70E sets the workplace requirements: risk assessment, approach boundaries, PPE, and equipment labelling.
--limits
The Lee equation is a theoretical open-air model and tends to be conservative. The Doughty equations used at 600 V and below are empirical, and valid only within the 16&ndash;50 kA range they were fitted to. Neither models electrode configuration, arc gap or enclosure size the way the IEEE 1584 arcing-current method does. This is a screening estimate only. Labelling and PPE selection require a full study by a qualified engineer.
--mistakes
Treating a screening number as a labelling result. Using a clearing time from the device curve without accounting for the arcing current being lower than bolted fault current, which can put the device on a slower part of its curve. Assuming higher fault current always means higher incident energy — sometimes it clears the device faster and lowers it.

===ampacity|ampacity|Cable Ampacity &amp; Conduit Fill Calculator|Cable Ampacity &amp; Conduit Fill Calculator|Look up NEC Table 310.16 conductor ampacity and check conduit fill against the 40% limit for three or more conductors. Free, no signup.
--intro
Ampacity is the current a conductor can carry continuously without exceeding its insulation temperature rating. The table value is where the calculation starts, not where it ends: the published figure assumes no more than three current-carrying conductors in a raceway at 30&deg;C ambient, and both of those assumptions fail routinely in real installations. Conduit fill is the companion check — too many conductors in a raceway makes them impossible to pull without damage and traps the heat that ampacity derating exists to manage.
--formula
Fill % = 100 &times; (n &times; A conductor) / A conduit
Limits: 53% for one conductor, 31% for two, 40% for three or more
--vars
n :: Number of conductors in the raceway
A conductor :: Cross-sectional area of one conductor including insulation
A conduit :: Internal cross-sectional area of the raceway
--example
Nine 12 AWG THHN conductors, each 0.0133 in&sup2;, in 3/4 inch EMT with 0.533 in&sup2; internal area:
Fill = 100 &times; (9 &times; 0.0133) / 0.533 = 22.5%
Inside the 40% limit. But nine current-carrying conductors trigger a 70% ampacity adjustment factor, so each one now carries far less than its table value.
--standard
Ampacity from NEC Table 310.16. Adjustment for more than three current-carrying conductors from 310.15(C)(1), ambient correction from 310.15(B). Conduit fill limits from NEC Chapter 9, Table 1, with conductor and raceway dimensions in Tables 4 and 5.
--limits
The lookup gives the base table value before any correction. Real ampacity is the table value multiplied by both the ambient correction factor and the conductor-count adjustment factor, and either can be substantial. Terminal temperature ratings under 110.14(C) may limit the answer further regardless of the conductor's own rating.
--mistakes
Using the 90&deg;C column when the terminations are only rated 75&deg;C — the termination governs. Counting the neutral as non-current-carrying on a circuit feeding non-linear load, where it carries harmonic current. Checking fill and forgetting the derating that the same conductor count triggers.

===bess-sizing|bess-sizing|BESS Sizing Calculator|BESS Sizing Calculator (kW and kWh)|Convert a critical load and backup duration into usable energy, then into the nameplate kWh you need once depth of discharge and round-trip efficiency are applied.
--intro
Battery storage is specified in two numbers that are easy to conflate: power in kW, which is how fast it can deliver, and energy in kWh, which is how long it can sustain that. The ratio between them is the C-rate, and it drives cell selection and thermal design. The other trap is that the energy you can use is not the energy on the nameplate — depth of discharge and round-trip efficiency both take a cut, and sizing to usable energy without grossing up for them under-sizes the system.
--formula
Usable kWh = kW &times; hours
Nameplate kWh = Usable kWh / (DoD &times; RTE)
--vars
kW :: Critical load the system must carry
hours :: Required backup duration
DoD :: Depth of discharge, the fraction of nameplate energy the BMS will allow you to use
RTE :: Round-trip efficiency, the fraction of energy in that comes back out
--example
A 500 kW load for 4 hours, at 90% depth of discharge and 88% round-trip efficiency:
Usable = 500 &times; 4 = 2,000 kWh
Nameplate = 2,000 / (0.90 &times; 0.88) = 2,525 kWh
So a 2,000 kWh requirement needs roughly 2,525 kWh of installed capacity on day one — and more than that at end of life, which is what augmentation plans for.
--standard
Installation requirements come from NFPA 855, with UL 9540 for the system and UL 9540A for the thermal-runaway fire test data that sets separation distances. Grid-interconnected inverters follow IEEE 1547 and UL 1741.
--limits
This is a day-one calculation. Capacity fades with cycling and calendar age, so a system sized exactly to requirement will miss it within a few years unless augmentation is planned. It also ignores auxiliary load — the thermal management system draws power from the same asset.
--mistakes
Sizing to usable energy and ordering that as nameplate. Ignoring degradation, then discovering the shortfall in year three. Treating power and energy as one specification, which produces a system that meets the kWh and cannot deliver the kW.

===solar-string|solar-string|Solar String Sizing Calculator|Solar String Sizing Calculator (Voc / Vmp)|Apply module temperature coefficients to site design temperatures to find maximum and minimum string length against inverter voltage limits. Free, no signup.
--intro
String length is set by temperature, not by the module's rated voltage. Open-circuit voltage rises as it gets colder, so the binding constraint is the coldest morning of the year: if cold-weather Voc exceeds the inverter's maximum DC input, the string damages the inverter. At the other end, maximum-power voltage falls as it gets hotter, so a string that is too short drops below the inverter's MPPT window on the hottest afternoon and stops tracking. The usable range sits between those two.
--formula
Voc(cold) = Voc &times; (1 + (Tc&middot;Voc / 100) &times; (Tmin &minus; 25))
Vmp(hot) = Vmp &times; (1 + (Tc&middot;Vmp / 100) &times; (Tmax &minus; 25))
--vars
Voc :: Module open-circuit voltage at standard test conditions
Vmp :: Module maximum-power voltage at STC
Tc&middot;Voc :: Temperature coefficient of Voc, percent per &deg;C, always negative
Tmin :: Record low ambient at the site
Tmax :: Design cell temperature, which runs well above ambient in full sun
--example
A module with Voc 49.5 V and a coefficient of &minus;0.27%/&deg;C, at a record low of &minus;15&deg;C:
Voc(cold) = 49.5 &times; (1 + (&minus;0.27/100) &times; (&minus;15 &minus; 25)) = 49.5 &times; 1.108 = 54.8 V
Against a 1500 V inverter limit: 1500 / 54.8 = 27.3, so 27 modules maximum.
Sizing on the 49.5 V nameplate instead would have suggested 30 — and exceeded the inverter limit on the first cold morning.
--standard
NEC 690.7 governs maximum PV system voltage and permits the temperature-coefficient method. Module coefficients come from the manufacturer's datasheet at STC per IEC 61215.
--limits
The result is only as good as the design temperatures. Record low is not average winter low, and cell temperature in full sun runs 20-30&deg;C above ambient. Uses module datasheet values, which carry their own tolerance.
--mistakes
Using average rather than record low temperature, which produces a string that is legal on paper and over-voltage in practice. Ignoring the hot-weather minimum entirely and building strings that fall out of the MPPT window in summer. Applying one site's temperatures to another project.

===power-factor|power-factor|Power Factor Correction Calculator|Power Factor Correction Calculator (kVAR)|Size the capacitor bank in kVAR needed to raise an existing power factor to a target, and see the effect on apparent power. Free, no signup.
--intro
Power factor is the ratio of real power to apparent power. A facility drawing 500 kW at 0.75 power factor is pulling 667 kVA through its transformer, cables and switchgear — a third more current than the real work requires, and every component in the path has to be sized for it. Correction adds capacitive reactive power locally so the reactive component stops being drawn through the whole system. Where the utility bills a power-factor penalty, the correction usually pays for itself on that alone.
--formula
kVAR = kW &times; (tan(arccos PF1) &minus; tan(arccos PF2))
--vars
kW :: Real power drawn by the load
PF1 :: Existing, uncorrected power factor
PF2 :: Target power factor after correction
kVAR :: Capacitor bank size required
--example
500 kW at 0.75 power factor, corrected to 0.95:
tan(arccos 0.75) = 0.882, tan(arccos 0.95) = 0.329
kVAR = 500 &times; (0.882 &minus; 0.329) = 277 kVAR
Apparent power falls from 667 kVA to 526 kVA — about 140 kVA of transformer and cable capacity released without adding a single kW of load.
--standard
IEEE Std 18 covers shunt power capacitors. NEC Article 460 governs capacitor installation, including the disconnect and overcurrent requirements.
--limits
This sizes a fixed bank for one operating point. Loads vary, and a fixed bank on a lightly loaded system can over-correct into a leading power factor, which causes its own voltage problems. It also assumes linear load: on a harmonic-rich system, capacitors can resonate with source inductance and amplify the harmonic instead of correcting anything.
--mistakes
Correcting to unity rather than to a target just below it, which risks over-correction as load varies. Installing capacitors on a harmonic-heavy bus without a detuning reactor. Sizing from a peak-demand reading and leaving the bank connected through the night.

===motor-start|motor-start|Motor Starting Voltage Dip Calculator|Motor Starting Voltage Dip Calculator|Estimate bus voltage dip when a large motor starts across the line, using locked-rotor kVA against source short-circuit kVA. Free, no signup.
--intro
A motor starting across the line draws locked-rotor current — commonly six times full load — and that inrush pulls the bus voltage down for as long as it takes to accelerate. If the dip is deep enough, contactors drop out, drives fault on undervoltage, and lighting visibly flickers. The screening question is simple: how stiff is the source relative to the motor's starting demand?
--formula
%Voltage dip = LRkVA / (LRkVA + SCkVA) &times; 100
--vars
LRkVA :: Locked-rotor kVA of the motor being started
SCkVA :: Short-circuit kVA available at the bus, a measure of source stiffness
--example
A motor with 1,200 kVA locked-rotor demand on a bus with 20,000 kVA short-circuit capacity:
Dip = 1200 / (1200 + 20000) &times; 100 = 5.7%
Acceptable for most equipment. Halve the source stiffness to 10,000 kVA and the dip becomes 10.7%, which is where contactor dropout and drive undervoltage trips start to appear.
--standard
The screening method is documented in IEEE Std 399 (the Brown Book). Motor locked-rotor codes are defined by NEMA MG 1, and the code letter on the nameplate is what gives you locked-rotor kVA per horsepower.
--limits
A steady-state screening estimate. It does not model the acceleration curve, the load torque the motor is starting against, or how long the dip persists — and duration matters as much as depth for whether a contactor actually drops out. Reduced-voltage starting and soft starters change the picture entirely.
--mistakes
Using full-load kVA instead of locked-rotor kVA, which understates the dip by a factor of about six. Ignoring dip duration. Assuming a generator-backed bus behaves like a utility source, when a generator is far softer and dips much further.

===inrush|inrush|Transformer Inrush Current Calculator|Transformer Inrush Current Calculator|Estimate magnetizing inrush current on transformer energisation as a multiple of full-load amps, for upstream breaker and fuse selection. Free, no signup.
--intro
Energising a transformer draws a magnetizing inrush that is far above full-load current — commonly 8 to 12 times, decaying over several cycles. It is not a fault, but an upstream protective device cannot tell the difference unless it has been selected to ride through it. Getting this wrong produces a transformer that trips its own feeder breaker every time it is energised.
--formula
FLA = kVA &times; 1000 / (1.732 &times; V)  [three-phase]
Peak inrush &asymp; M &times; FLA
--vars
kVA :: Transformer nameplate rating
V :: Line-to-line voltage on the side being energised
M :: Inrush multiplier, typically 8 to 12 for modern units
--example
A 1000 kVA transformer energised from a 480 V bus:
FLA = 1000 &times; 1000 / (1.732 &times; 480) = 1,203 A
At a multiplier of 10, peak inrush is roughly 12,000 A for the first cycle.
The upstream device must not see that as a fault, which usually means an instantaneous setting above it or a deliberately delayed curve.
--standard
IEEE C57.12.00 covers transformer requirements. Overcurrent protection limits for transformers are set by NEC Article 450, which permits higher primary protection specifically to allow for inrush.
--limits
The multiplier is an estimate, not a measurement. Actual inrush depends on residual core flux and the exact point on the voltage wave at which the switch closes, and worst-case energisation can exceed the typical figure. Magnitude also decays over several cycles, so a single peak number does not describe the whole event.
--mistakes
Setting instantaneous protection from full-load current alone. Assuming inrush is the same on every energisation when it depends on residual flux from the last de-energisation. Applying a low multiplier to a modern low-loss core, which tends to have higher inrush than older designs.

===grounding|grounding|Ground Rod Resistance Calculator|Ground Rod Resistance Calculator (Dwight's Formula)|Estimate resistance-to-earth of a driven ground rod from soil resistivity, rod length and diameter, using Dwight's formula. Free, no signup.
--intro
The resistance of a driven rod to earth is dominated by soil resistivity and by rod length — and much less than people expect by rod diameter. Doubling length roughly halves resistance; doubling diameter changes it by only a few percent. That is why grounding designs add rods and depth rather than thicker rods, and why the NEC's 25-ohm threshold is met with a second electrode rather than a bigger one.
--formula
R = [&rho; / (2&pi;L)] &times; [ln(4L/d) &minus; 1]
--vars
&rho; :: Soil resistivity in ohm-metres, which varies enormously: wet clay is tens, dry sand or rock is thousands
L :: Rod length in metres
d :: Rod diameter in metres
R :: Resistance to earth in ohms
--example
A 3 m rod, 16 mm diameter, in 100 ohm-metre soil:
R = [100 / (2&pi; &times; 3)] &times; [ln(4 &times; 3 / 0.016) &minus; 1]
R = 5.31 &times; (6.62 &minus; 1) = 29.8 ohms
Above the 25-ohm figure, so a second electrode is required. Doubling the rod to 6 m brings it to roughly 17 ohms; doubling the diameter instead would barely move it.
--standard
NEC 250.53(A)(2) requires a supplemental electrode where a single rod exceeds 25 ohms. IEEE Std 81 covers measurement of earth resistivity and ground resistance; IEEE Std 80 covers substation grounding design.
--limits
Dwight's formula assumes uniform soil, which real ground is not — resistivity varies with depth, moisture and season, and a summer measurement can be far better than the same rod in a dry autumn. It models one rod: multiple rods interact, and their combined resistance is higher than simple parallel arithmetic suggests unless they are spaced at least one rod length apart.
--mistakes
Treating a calculation as a substitute for measurement, when the code requirement is about measured resistance. Specifying a larger diameter to hit a resistance target. Spacing multiple rods too closely, so their shells of influence overlap and the array performs worse than expected.

--formula

===amps-to-kw|amps-to-kw|Amps to kW Calculator|Amps to kW Calculator (1-Phase, 3-Phase, DC)|Convert amps to kilowatts for single-phase, three-phase and DC circuits, with the power factor term that makes the difference. Free, no signup.
--intro
Amps measure current. Kilowatts measure real power &mdash; the part actually doing work. Converting between them needs the voltage, and on an AC circuit it also needs the power factor, because current and voltage are not in step. Leave power factor out and the answer comes back high by however far the load sits from unity, which on a motor-heavy feeder is 10 to 20 percent.
The three-phase case carries an extra factor of the square root of three. That term is not a fudge: it falls out of three voltages 120 degrees apart, and it is the single most commonly dropped part of this calculation.
--formula
kW = (V &times; I &times; PF) / 1000  [single-phase]
kW = (1.732 &times; V &times; I &times; PF) / 1000  [three-phase]
kW = (V &times; I) / 1000  [DC]
--vars
V :: Voltage. Line-to-line for three-phase, line-to-neutral for single-phase
I :: Current in amperes
PF :: Power factor, between 0 and 1. Unity on a purely resistive load; roughly 0.8 to 0.9 on a motor
1.732 :: The square root of three, which appears in every three-phase power calculation
--example
A 100 A three-phase load at 480 V with a power factor of 0.9:
kW = (1.732 &times; 480 &times; 100 &times; 0.9) / 1000 = 74.8 kW
The same 100 A single-phase at 240 V, purely resistive:
kW = (240 &times; 100 &times; 1.0) / 1000 = 24 kW
Same current, very different power. The voltage and the number of phases do most of the work here.
--standard
There is no code section governing the arithmetic; it is the definition of real power. The place a standard enters is the current you put in: NEC Article 220 covers how load current is calculated for sizing purposes, and NEC 430.250 gives the full-load current tables for motors rather than letting you derive them.
--limits
This returns real power at the operating point you describe. It is not a demand figure, and it says nothing about what happens while a motor starts, where current can run six to eight times full-load for a few seconds. On a non-linear load &mdash; drives, rectifiers, switch-mode supplies &mdash; a single power factor also hides the distortion component, and the true power factor is lower than the displacement figure a meter reports.
--mistakes
Dropping the square root of three on a three-phase circuit, which overstates power by 73 percent. Using unity power factor because the real figure is unknown, which overstates it again. Mixing line-to-line and line-to-neutral voltage on the same calculation. Reading nameplate amps as operating amps: a nameplate states full-load current, and most equipment does not run there.

===kw-to-amps|kw-to-amps|kW to Amps Calculator|kW to Amps Calculator (1-Phase, 3-Phase, DC)|Convert kilowatts to amps for single-phase, three-phase and DC circuits, and get the current a conductor actually has to carry. Free, no signup.
--intro
This is the direction that matters for sizing. Equipment is specified in kilowatts and conductors are sized in amperes, so every feeder calculation starts by turning one into the other. The current you get out is the figure that drives conductor ampacity, overcurrent device rating and voltage drop.
Power factor works the opposite way here: a lower power factor means more current for the same kilowatts, not less. That is the whole reason power factor correction pays for itself &mdash; the real power is unchanged, but the current the system carries falls.
--formula
I = (kW &times; 1000) / (V &times; PF)  [single-phase]
I = (kW &times; 1000) / (1.732 &times; V &times; PF)  [three-phase]
I = (kW &times; 1000) / V  [DC]
--vars
kW :: Real power
V :: Voltage. Line-to-line for three-phase
PF :: Power factor, between 0 and 1
I :: Resulting current in amperes
--example
A 75 kW three-phase load at 480 V, power factor 0.9:
I = (75 &times; 1000) / (1.732 &times; 480 &times; 0.9) = 100.2 A
Drop the power factor to 0.75 and the same 75 kW draws 120.2 A &mdash; twenty percent more current for identical work, which can be the difference between one conductor size and the next.
--standard
The arithmetic is definitional. What the result feeds into is not: NEC 210.19 and 215.2 govern conductor sizing for branch circuits and feeders, NEC 310.16 gives the ampacity tables, and NEC 210.20 and 215.3 set how the overcurrent device relates to the calculated load. For continuous loads, the 125 percent rule in NEC 210.19(A) applies on top of this figure.
--limits
This is steady-state current at one operating point. It does not apply the 125 percent continuous-load factor, add any demand factor from NEC Article 220, or account for starting current. It also assumes balanced three-phase load; on an unbalanced system the phase currents differ and the highest one is what sizes the conductor.
--mistakes
Sizing a conductor on this number alone, with no continuous-load factor and no derating for ambient temperature or conduit fill. Forgetting that the overcurrent device, not the calculated current, is what the conductor must coordinate with. Using the same power factor for a resistive and a motor load. Assuming three-phase current is simply one third of the single-phase figure, which it is not.

===kva-to-amps|kva-to-amps|kVA to Amps Calculator|kVA to Amps Calculator (Transformer Full-Load Amps)|Convert a kVA rating to full-load amps for single-phase and three-phase transformers. No power factor needed, and this page explains why. Free, no signup.
--intro
Transformers, generators and UPS systems are rated in kVA &mdash; apparent power. Converting a kVA rating to amperes gives full-load current, the figure that sizes secondary conductors, sets the frame of the main device and tells you whether existing gear can carry the unit.
The useful thing about this conversion is what it does not need: power factor. kVA is already apparent power, the product of volts and amps, so the power factor term that belongs in every kW calculation has no place here. If you find yourself dividing by power factor, you are converting kW, not kVA.
--formula
I = (kVA &times; 1000) / V  [single-phase]
I = (kVA &times; 1000) / (1.732 &times; V)  [three-phase]
--vars
kVA :: Apparent power, the nameplate rating
V :: Voltage on the side you are calculating. Line-to-line for three-phase
I :: Full-load amperes on that side
--example
A 500 kVA transformer with a 480 V three-phase secondary:
I = (500 &times; 1000) / (1.732 &times; 480) = 601 A
On the 13.8 kV primary the same 500 kVA is only 20.9 A, which is why primary conductors are small and secondary conductors are not. Calculate each side separately; the kVA is the same on both, the current is not.
--standard
Standard kVA nameplate ratings come from ANSI/IEEE C57.12.00. How far a transformer may be loaded beyond nameplate, and for how long, is covered by IEEE C57.91. Secondary conductor and overcurrent protection rules are in NEC Article 450 for the transformer itself and Article 240 for the protection.

This is full-load current at nameplate rating, not the current your load actually draws. It also says nothing about inrush, which on energization can reach eight to twenty-five times this figure for a few cycles, or about fault current, which depends on the transformer's impedance rather than its rating.
--mistakes
Dividing by power factor, which belongs in kW calculations and not here. Calculating one side and assuming the other scales with voltage in the wrong direction. Treating full-load amps as operating amps when sizing cooling or estimating losses. Forgetting that a transformer's ability to deliver fault current comes from %Z, so a correctly sized conductor can still sit downstream of under-rated switchgear.

===hp-to-kw|hp-to-kw|Motor HP to kW Calculator|Motor HP to kW Calculator (and HP to Amps)|Convert motor horsepower to kW output, to kW drawn from the supply, and to full-load amps, with the efficiency term most converters leave out. Free, no signup.
--intro
Horsepower on a motor nameplate is mechanical output at the shaft, not electrical input. One horsepower is 745.7 watts, so the conversion to kilowatts looks trivial &mdash; and it is, as long as you are clear which side of the motor you mean.
The number that matters for sizing is input power, which is higher, because the motor is not perfectly efficient. A 50 hp motor does not draw 37 kW; it draws 37 kW divided by its efficiency. Converters that stop at the 0.7457 multiplier give you the shaft figure and let you under-size the supply.
--formula
kW (shaft output) = HP &times; 0.7457
kW (drawn from supply) = (HP &times; 0.7457) / &eta;
I = (HP &times; 745.7) / (1.732 &times; V &times; PF &times; &eta;)  [three-phase]
--vars
HP :: Nameplate horsepower, mechanical output
0.7457 :: Watts per horsepower, divided by a thousand. 1 hp = 745.7 W
&eta; :: Efficiency as a decimal. Commonly 0.90 to 0.96 on a modern premium-efficiency motor
PF :: Power factor at full load, typically 0.80 to 0.88 on an induction motor
--example
A 50 hp motor at 460 V three-phase, 93 percent efficient, power factor 0.86:
Shaft output = 50 &times; 0.7457 = 37.3 kW
Drawn from the supply = 37.3 / 0.93 = 40.1 kW
Current = (50 &times; 745.7) / (1.732 &times; 460 &times; 0.86 &times; 0.93) = 58.5 A
Worth comparing: NEC Table 430.250 lists 65 A for a 50 hp motor at 460 V. The code table is deliberately conservative and is what you size conductors and protection from, not the calculated figure. Use the calculation to understand the motor; use the table to size the circuit.
--standard
NEC 430.250 gives the full-load current tables that must be used for conductor and overcurrent sizing, and NEC 430.22 sets the 125 percent factor on top. Motor efficiency classes are defined by NEMA MG1 in North America and IEC 60034-30-1 internationally. The 745.7 W per horsepower figure is the mechanical definition, not a convention.
--limits
This covers steady-state operation at full load. It says nothing about starting: locked-rotor current typically runs six to eight times full-load amps, which is what sizes a soft starter or sets the voltage dip on the bus. Efficiency and power factor both fall at part load, so a motor running at 40 percent load draws proportionally more current than this suggests.
--mistakes
Converting horsepower to kilowatts and treating the result as input power, which under-states the supply by the efficiency. Sizing conductors from the calculated current instead of the NEC 430.250 table. Using full-load power factor for a motor that spends its life lightly loaded. Ignoring starting current entirely, which is how a correctly sized feeder still trips on inrush.

===wire-size|ampacity|Wire Size Calculator|Wire Size Calculator (What Size Wire for X Amps)|Size a conductor properly: the 125% continuous-load factor, the termination temperature limit under NEC 110.14(C), and ambient and bundling adjustment. Free, no signup.
--intro
"What size wire for 100 amps" has no single answer, which is why the charts that give one are so often wrong. The conductor has to satisfy two separate checks, and the answer is whichever is larger.
The first is the termination temperature. Equipment terminals are listed for 60&deg;C or 75&deg;C, and NEC 110.14(C) says the whole circuit is limited by the lowest-rated termination in it &mdash; so a 90&deg;C conductor landing on a 75&deg;C lug is sized from the 75&deg;C column no matter what the insulation can take. The second is the conductor's own derating: ambient temperature and the number of current-carrying conductors bundled together both reduce what it can safely carry, and that calculation runs on the insulation's own column.
--formula
Required ampacity = (continuous load &times; 1.25) + non-continuous load
Derated ampacity = base ampacity at the insulation rating &times; ambient factor &times; bundling factor
Conductor = the larger of the two results
--vars
Continuous load :: Any load expected to run three hours or more, per NEC 210.19(A)
Termination rating :: 60 or 75&deg;C, from the equipment listing, per NEC 110.14(C)
Ambient factor :: From NEC 310.15(B)(1), applied when ambient is not 30&deg;C
Bundling factor :: From NEC 310.15(C)(1), applied for four or more current-carrying conductors
--example
A 100 A continuous load, copper, 75&deg;C terminations, in a 40&deg;C attic with six current-carrying conductors in the raceway:
Termination check: 100 &times; 1.25 = 125 A required. In the 75&deg;C copper column, 1 AWG carries 130 A. 1 AWG passes.
Derating check: 1 AWG copper at 90&deg;C is 145 A. At 40&deg;C ambient the factor is 0.91, and six conductors gives 0.80: 145 &times; 0.91 &times; 0.80 = 105.6 A, against an actual load of 100 A. 1 AWG passes again.
Both checks land on 1 AWG, so that is the answer. Change the ambient to 50&deg;C and the derating check gives 145 &times; 0.82 &times; 0.80 = 95.1 A, below the load, and the conductor goes up to 1/0 while the termination check has not moved at all.
--standard
NEC 110.14(C) sets the termination temperature limitation. NEC 210.19(A) and 215.2(A) give the 125 percent continuous-load factor for branch circuits and feeders. Base ampacities are in NEC Table 310.16, ambient correction in 310.15(B)(1) and bundling adjustment in 310.15(C)(1). Overcurrent device selection follows NEC 240.4 and the standard ratings in 240.6.
--limits
This sizes for ampacity only. It does not check voltage drop, which on a long run is frequently what forces the conductor up rather than heat, and it does not check short-circuit withstand. It covers conductors in a raceway or cable at a single ambient; buried runs, conductors in sunlight on a rooftop and conductors passing through different ambients each have their own rules. It also does not handle motor circuits, where NEC Article 430 requires the table full-load current rather than the actual load, or the 83 percent allowance in 310.12 for certain dwelling services.
--mistakes
Sizing from the 90&deg;C column because the wire is THHN, when the terminals are listed for 75&deg;C. Applying the 125 percent factor to the derated figure rather than running the two checks separately. Forgetting that the neutral counts as a current-carrying conductor on a circuit with significant harmonic content. Counting only the conductors of one circuit when several share the raceway. Treating a chart found online as authoritative when it states neither the termination temperature nor the ambient it assumed.

===awg-to-mm2|awg-to-mm2|AWG to mm&sup2; Converter|AWG to mm&sup2; Converter (Wire Size Conversion)|Convert AWG and kcmil to square millimetres and back, with the nearest IEC standard size. Built from the AWG geometric definition. Free, no signup.
--intro
AWG is not an arbitrary list of sizes. It is a geometric progression, and knowing that is the difference between looking a number up and understanding what it means: the diameter of gauge n is 0.005 inch multiplied by 92 raised to the power (36 minus n) over 39. Every size in the system falls out of that one expression.
Two consequences follow immediately. The familiar rules of thumb are exact properties of the definition rather than approximations &mdash; six gauge steps is almost precisely four times the area, three steps almost precisely double. And the gauge numbers run backwards, because they originally counted the number of drawing dies the wire was pulled through, so a larger number means a thinner conductor. Above 4/0 the system runs out and sizes are given in thousands of circular mils instead.
--formula
Diameter (in) = 0.005 &times; 92<sup>(36 &minus; n)/39</sup>, where n is the gauge number and 4/0 is n = &minus;3
Circular mils = (diameter in mils)<sup>2</sup>
Area (mm&sup2;) = circular mils &times; 0.0005067
--vars
n :: Gauge number. 1/0 is 0, 2/0 is &minus;1, 3/0 is &minus;2, 4/0 is &minus;3
Circular mil :: The area of a circle one mil (0.001 inch) in diameter
kcmil :: One thousand circular mils, used for every size above 4/0
IEC size :: A standard metric cross-section from the series 1.5, 2.5, 4, 6, 10, 16, 25 mm&sup2; and upward
--example
Take 12 AWG. The gauge number is 12, so the diameter is 0.005 &times; 92<sup>24/39</sup> = 0.0808 inch, which is 80.8 mils.
Squaring that gives 6,530 circular mils, the figure NEC Chapter 9 Table 8 lists for 12 AWG. Multiplying by 0.0005067 gives 3.31 mm&sup2;.
The nearest IEC standard size is 4 mm&sup2;, which is 21 percent larger. There is no metric size that equals 12 AWG, which is the single most important thing this conversion tells you.
Checking the rule of thumb: six steps up from 12 AWG is 6 AWG, at 26,240 circular mils. That is 4.02 times the area of 12 AWG, not exactly four, because the ratio is 92 raised to the power 12 over 39.
--standard
The AWG geometric definition is the basis of the conductor sizes in ASTM B258. NEC Chapter 9 Table 8 gives the standardized conductor properties used for code work in the United States, including circular mil area, cross-sectional area in square millimetres, and DC resistance for both solid and stranded constructions. IEC 60228 defines the metric conductor classes and their standard cross-sections. Ampacity comes from NEC Table 310.16 and its adjustment rules, or from IEC 60364-5-52 outside North America.
--limits
This converts area and nothing else. It does not convert ampacity, and the two are not interchangeable: NEC and IEC rate conductors under different installation methods, ambient references and grouping assumptions, so two conductors of equal cross-section can carry different permitted currents under the two systems. The areas given are the geometric definition for a solid conductor, so a stranded conductor of the same size has a larger overall diameter and a slightly different standardized area, and conduit fill must use the actual overall diameter rather than the conductor area. It also says nothing about insulation thickness, voltage rating, temperature rating or stranding class, any of which can decide whether a nominally equivalent conductor is actually a substitute.
--mistakes
Treating the nearest metric size as an equivalent when it is usually several percent away, and substituting downward without noticing. Converting an ampacity figure along with the area. Using the conductor area for conduit fill instead of the overall insulated diameter. Assuming the gauge numbers increase with size. Reading a cmil figure as if it were a mm&sup2; figure, which differs by a factor of about 1,974. Specifying a metric conductor for a North American installation without checking that the terminations are listed for it.
