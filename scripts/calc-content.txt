===voltage-drop|voltage-drop|Voltage Drop Calculator|Voltage Drop Calculator (Cable Sizing)|Calculate voltage drop on a feeder using the K-factor method, check it against the 3% and 5% design limits, and size the conductor. Free, no signup.
--intro
Voltage drop is the loss along a conductor between the source and the load. It is not a code violation in itself — the NEC states its 3% branch-circuit and 5% total figures as recommendations in informational notes, not requirements — but equipment is rated to operate within a voltage band, and a long run that drops too far will leave a motor starting hard, a contactor chattering, or a drive faulting on undervoltage. On a long feeder it is usually voltage drop, not ampacity, that forces the conductor up a size.
--formula
VD = (2 &times; K &times; I &times; D) / CM  [single-phase]
VD = (1.732 &times; K &times; I &times; D) / CM  [three-phase]
--vars
K :: Resistivity constant: 12.9 for copper, 21.2 for aluminium, in ohm-cmil/ft
I :: Load current in amperes
D :: One-way circuit length in feet
CM :: Conductor cross-section in circular mils
2 or 1.732 :: Accounts for the return path: two conductors single-phase, the square root of three for three-phase
--example
A 100 A three-phase load at 480 V, 150 ft away, on 2 AWG copper (66,360 cmil):
VD = (1.732 &times; 12.9 &times; 100 &times; 150) / 66,360 = 5.05 V
As a percentage: 5.05 / 480 = 1.05%. Comfortably inside 3%.
Move the same load to 400 ft and it becomes 13.5 V, or 2.8% — still inside the limit, but with nothing left for the branch circuit beyond it.
--standard
The K-factor method is the standard hand calculation. NEC 210.19(A) and 215.2(A) informational notes give the 3% branch and 5% total figures. For exact work, IEEE Std 141 (the Red Book) covers the full method with reactance included.
--limits
This is a resistance-only calculation. It ignores conductor reactance, which matters on larger conductors and longer runs — above roughly 250 ft, or above about 4/0, the reactive component starts to dominate and this method under-reads. It also assumes a 75&deg;C conductor temperature and a balanced load.
--mistakes
Using one-way distance where the formula wants it and then doubling it again — the 2 and the 1.732 already account for the return path. Sizing to ampacity first and never checking drop on a long run. Applying the single-phase formula to a three-phase circuit, which over-states drop by about 15%.
--related

===transformer-sizing|transformer-sizing|Transformer Sizing Calculator|Transformer Sizing Calculator (kW to kVA)|Convert a kW load to the kVA rating you need, round to the nearest standard ANSI/IEEE C57.12.00 nameplate, and get full-load amps. Free, no signup.
--intro
Transformers are rated in kVA — apparent power — while loads are usually described in kW, real power. The difference is power factor, and ignoring it is how a transformer ends up undersized on day one. Once you have the kVA figure you cannot simply order it: transformers are built to a standard series of nameplate ratings, so the practical answer is always the next size up.
--formula
kVA = kW / PF
FLA = kVA &times; 1000 / (1.732 &times; V)  [three-phase]
FLA = kVA &times; 1000 / V  [single-phase]
--vars
kW :: Real power drawn by the load
PF :: Power factor, between 0 and 1
V :: Line-to-line secondary voltage
FLA :: Full-load amperes at the nameplate rating
--example
A 400 kW load at 0.85 power factor:
kVA = 400 / 0.85 = 470.6 kVA
The standard series runs 300, 500, 750, 1000 — so the unit specified is 500 kVA.
At 480 V three-phase: FLA = 500 &times; 1000 / (1.732 &times; 480) = 601 A.
--standard
Standard nameplate ratings come from ANSI/IEEE C57.12.00. Loading practice beyond nameplate is covered by IEEE C57.91, which governs how far and how long a unit may be run over rating before insulation life is affected.
--limits
This sizes for steady-state load only. It does not account for future growth, harmonic heating, non-linear load, or motor starting, all of which argue for headroom. It also says nothing about impedance, which is the other half of a transformer specification and the one that sets downstream fault current.
--mistakes
Sizing on kW and never dividing by power factor, which under-sizes by however far PF sits below unity. Sizing exactly to calculated load with no growth allowance. Choosing on kVA alone and leaving %Z to the supplier, which is how switchgear ends up under-rated for the fault current the transformer can deliver.

===fault-current|fault-current|Available Fault Current Calculator|Fault Current Calculator (Short Circuit)|Estimate available symmetrical fault current at a transformer secondary from kVA, voltage and %Z, and check equipment withstand ratings. Free, no signup.
--intro
Available fault current is how much current the system can deliver into a bolted short circuit at a given point. It sets the interrupting rating every protective device downstream must meet, and the withstand rating of the gear that has to survive the event. The counter-intuitive part: a lower transformer impedance means a stiffer source and therefore a higher fault current, so specifying a low-%Z transformer makes the downstream equipment problem worse, not better.
--formula
Isc = (100,000 &times; kVA) / (1.732 &times; V &times; %Z)
--vars
kVA :: Transformer nameplate rating
V :: Line-to-line secondary voltage
%Z :: Transformer impedance as a percentage of rated voltage
Isc :: Symmetrical RMS fault current in amperes
--example
A 1000 kVA transformer at 480 V with 5.75% impedance:
Isc = (100,000 &times; 1000) / (1.732 &times; 480 &times; 5.75) = 20,919 A
So roughly 21 kA at the secondary terminals, and the main breaker needs an interrupting rating above that. Drop the impedance to 4% and the same transformer delivers about 30 kA.
--standard
IEEE Std 141 covers the full method. For arc-flash and coordination work the governing analysis is IEEE 1584. Equipment interrupting ratings are marked per UL 489 for moulded-case breakers and ANSI C37 for power breakers.
--limits
This is an infinite-source estimate: it assumes the utility can supply unlimited current into the transformer primary, which over-states the real figure — conservative, and therefore safe for a first pass. It ignores cable impedance between the transformer and the point of interest, which reduces fault current with distance, and it ignores motor contribution, which increases it for the first few cycles.
--mistakes
Using the calculated value as a final number instead of commissioning a study. Forgetting motor contribution on a motor-heavy bus. Assuming a lower impedance is always better. Applying the secondary-terminal figure to equipment far downstream, where the real value is lower.

===arc-flash|arc-flash|Arc-Flash Incident Energy Calculator|Arc-Flash Incident Energy Calculator|Estimate arc-flash incident energy and boundary with the Lee and Doughty equations, as a first pass before a full IEEE 1584 study. Free, no signup.
--intro
Incident energy is the thermal energy an arc would deliver to a surface at a working distance, in calories per square centimetre. It determines the PPE category and the arc-flash boundary that must appear on the equipment label. Two inputs dominate it, and only one is obvious: available fault current, and how long the upstream device takes to clear. Halving clearing time roughly halves incident energy, which is why protection settings matter more to arc-flash exposure than almost anything else.
--formula
Above 600 V the Lee equation models an open-air arc; at 600 V and below the Doughty equations cover open-air and arc-in-a-box cases. In all of them, incident energy is directly proportional to arcing time, rises with available fault current, and falls steeply with working distance.
--vars
Bolted fault current :: From the fault-current calculation at that point
Clearing time :: How long the upstream protective device takes to interrupt, in seconds
Working distance :: Distance from the arc to the worker's face and chest, commonly 18 inches for LV gear
System voltage :: Above 600 V the calculator uses the Lee equation; at 600 V and below, the Doughty equations. Both are from NFPA 70E Annex D
--example
At 480 V in an enclosure, with 22.6 kA bolted fault current and an 18 in working distance, the Doughty equation gives 4.3 cal/cm&sup2; at 0.1 s clearing and 8.5 cal/cm&sup2; at 0.2 s. Double the time, double the energy: incident energy is directly proportional to arcing time. The arc-flash boundary grows less, from about 3.6 ft to 5.7 ft, because energy falls off steeply with distance. A breaker setting change that shaves 100 ms off clearing can move a task into a lower PPE category without touching the hardware.
--standard
The Lee and Doughty equations are documented in NFPA 70E Annex D. IEEE 1584 is the governing standard for a full arc-flash calculation. NFPA 70E sets the workplace requirements: risk assessment, approach boundaries, PPE, and equipment labelling.
--limits
The Lee equation is a theoretical open-air model and tends to be conservative. The Doughty equations used at 600 V and below are empirical, and valid only within the 16&ndash;50 kA range they were fitted to. Neither models electrode configuration, arc gap or enclosure size the way the IEEE 1584 arcing-current method does. This is a screening estimate only. Labelling and PPE selection require a full study by a qualified engineer.
--mistakes
Treating a screening number as a labelling result. Using a clearing time from the device curve without accounting for the arcing current being lower than bolted fault current, which can put the device on a slower part of its curve. Assuming higher fault current always means higher incident energy — sometimes it clears the device faster and lowers it.

===ampacity|ampacity|Cable Ampacity &amp; Conduit Fill Calculator|Cable Ampacity &amp; Conduit Fill Calculator|Look up NEC Table 310.16 conductor ampacity and check conduit fill against the 40% limit for three or more conductors. Free, no signup.
--intro
Ampacity is the current a conductor can carry continuously without exceeding its insulation temperature rating. The table value is where the calculation starts, not where it ends: the published figure assumes no more than three current-carrying conductors in a raceway at 30&deg;C ambient, and both of those assumptions fail routinely in real installations. Conduit fill is the companion check — too many conductors in a raceway makes them impossible to pull without damage and traps the heat that ampacity derating exists to manage.
--formula
Fill % = 100 &times; (n &times; A conductor) / A conduit
Limits: 53% for one conductor, 31% for two, 40% for three or more
--vars
n :: Number of conductors in the raceway
A conductor :: Cross-sectional area of one conductor including insulation
A conduit :: Internal cross-sectional area of the raceway
--example
Nine 12 AWG THHN conductors, each 0.0133 in&sup2;, in 3/4 inch EMT with 0.533 in&sup2; internal area:
Fill = 100 &times; (9 &times; 0.0133) / 0.533 = 22.5%
Inside the 40% limit. But nine current-carrying conductors trigger a 70% ampacity adjustment factor, so each one now carries far less than its table value.
--standard
Ampacity from NEC Table 310.16. Adjustment for more than three current-carrying conductors from 310.15(C)(1), ambient correction from 310.15(B). Conduit fill limits from NEC Chapter 9, Table 1, with conductor and raceway dimensions in Tables 4 and 5.
--limits
The lookup gives the base table value before any correction. Real ampacity is the table value multiplied by both the ambient correction factor and the conductor-count adjustment factor, and either can be substantial. Terminal temperature ratings under 110.14(C) may limit the answer further regardless of the conductor's own rating.
--mistakes
Using the 90&deg;C column when the terminations are only rated 75&deg;C — the termination governs. Counting the neutral as non-current-carrying on a circuit feeding non-linear load, where it carries harmonic current. Checking fill and forgetting the derating that the same conductor count triggers.

===bess-sizing|bess-sizing|BESS Sizing Calculator|BESS Sizing Calculator (kW and kWh)|Convert a critical load and backup duration into usable energy, then into the nameplate kWh you need once depth of discharge and round-trip efficiency are applied.
--intro
Battery storage is specified in two numbers that are easy to conflate: power in kW, which is how fast it can deliver, and energy in kWh, which is how long it can sustain that. The ratio between them is the C-rate, and it drives cell selection and thermal design. The other trap is that the energy you can use is not the energy on the nameplate — depth of discharge and round-trip efficiency both take a cut, and sizing to usable energy without grossing up for them under-sizes the system.
--formula
Usable kWh = kW &times; hours
Nameplate kWh = Usable kWh / (DoD &times; RTE)
--vars
kW :: Critical load the system must carry
hours :: Required backup duration
DoD :: Depth of discharge, the fraction of nameplate energy the BMS will allow you to use
RTE :: Round-trip efficiency, the fraction of energy in that comes back out
--example
A 500 kW load for 4 hours, at 90% depth of discharge and 88% round-trip efficiency:
Usable = 500 &times; 4 = 2,000 kWh
Nameplate = 2,000 / (0.90 &times; 0.88) = 2,525 kWh
So a 2,000 kWh requirement needs roughly 2,525 kWh of installed capacity on day one — and more than that at end of life, which is what augmentation plans for.
--standard
Installation requirements come from NFPA 855, with UL 9540 for the system and UL 9540A for the thermal-runaway fire test data that sets separation distances. Grid-interconnected inverters follow IEEE 1547 and UL 1741.
--limits
This is a day-one calculation. Capacity fades with cycling and calendar age, so a system sized exactly to requirement will miss it within a few years unless augmentation is planned. It also ignores auxiliary load — the thermal management system draws power from the same asset.
--mistakes
Sizing to usable energy and ordering that as nameplate. Ignoring degradation, then discovering the shortfall in year three. Treating power and energy as one specification, which produces a system that meets the kWh and cannot deliver the kW.

===solar-string|solar-string|Solar String Sizing Calculator|Solar String Sizing Calculator (Voc / Vmp)|Apply module temperature coefficients to site design temperatures to find maximum and minimum string length against inverter voltage limits. Free, no signup.
--intro
String length is set by temperature, not by the module's rated voltage. Open-circuit voltage rises as it gets colder, so the binding constraint is the coldest morning of the year: if cold-weather Voc exceeds the inverter's maximum DC input, the string damages the inverter. At the other end, maximum-power voltage falls as it gets hotter, so a string that is too short drops below the inverter's MPPT window on the hottest afternoon and stops tracking. The usable range sits between those two.
--formula
Voc(cold) = Voc &times; (1 + (Tc&middot;Voc / 100) &times; (Tmin &minus; 25))
Vmp(hot) = Vmp &times; (1 + (Tc&middot;Vmp / 100) &times; (Tmax &minus; 25))
--vars
Voc :: Module open-circuit voltage at standard test conditions
Vmp :: Module maximum-power voltage at STC
Tc&middot;Voc :: Temperature coefficient of Voc, percent per &deg;C, always negative
Tmin :: Record low ambient at the site
Tmax :: Design cell temperature, which runs well above ambient in full sun
--example
A module with Voc 49.5 V and a coefficient of &minus;0.27%/&deg;C, at a record low of &minus;15&deg;C:
Voc(cold) = 49.5 &times; (1 + (&minus;0.27/100) &times; (&minus;15 &minus; 25)) = 49.5 &times; 1.108 = 54.8 V
Against a 1500 V inverter limit: 1500 / 54.8 = 27.3, so 27 modules maximum.
Sizing on the 49.5 V nameplate instead would have suggested 30 — and exceeded the inverter limit on the first cold morning.
--standard
NEC 690.7 governs maximum PV system voltage and permits the temperature-coefficient method. Module coefficients come from the manufacturer's datasheet at STC per IEC 61215.
--limits
The result is only as good as the design temperatures. Record low is not average winter low, and cell temperature in full sun runs 20-30&deg;C above ambient. Uses module datasheet values, which carry their own tolerance.
--mistakes
Using average rather than record low temperature, which produces a string that is legal on paper and over-voltage in practice. Ignoring the hot-weather minimum entirely and building strings that fall out of the MPPT window in summer. Applying one site's temperatures to another project.

===power-factor|power-factor|Power Factor Correction Calculator|Power Factor Correction Calculator (kVAR)|Size the capacitor bank in kVAR needed to raise an existing power factor to a target, and see the effect on apparent power. Free, no signup.
--intro
Power factor is the ratio of real power to apparent power. A facility drawing 500 kW at 0.75 power factor is pulling 667 kVA through its transformer, cables and switchgear — a third more current than the real work requires, and every component in the path has to be sized for it. Correction adds capacitive reactive power locally so the reactive component stops being drawn through the whole system. Where the utility bills a power-factor penalty, the correction usually pays for itself on that alone.
--formula
kVAR = kW &times; (tan(arccos PF1) &minus; tan(arccos PF2))
--vars
kW :: Real power drawn by the load
PF1 :: Existing, uncorrected power factor
PF2 :: Target power factor after correction
kVAR :: Capacitor bank size required
--example
500 kW at 0.75 power factor, corrected to 0.95:
tan(arccos 0.75) = 0.882, tan(arccos 0.95) = 0.329
kVAR = 500 &times; (0.882 &minus; 0.329) = 277 kVAR
Apparent power falls from 667 kVA to 526 kVA — about 140 kVA of transformer and cable capacity released without adding a single kW of load.
--standard
IEEE Std 18 covers shunt power capacitors. NEC Article 460 governs capacitor installation, including the disconnect and overcurrent requirements.
--limits
This sizes a fixed bank for one operating point. Loads vary, and a fixed bank on a lightly loaded system can over-correct into a leading power factor, which causes its own voltage problems. It also assumes linear load: on a harmonic-rich system, capacitors can resonate with source inductance and amplify the harmonic instead of correcting anything.
--mistakes
Correcting to unity rather than to a target just below it, which risks over-correction as load varies. Installing capacitors on a harmonic-heavy bus without a detuning reactor. Sizing from a peak-demand reading and leaving the bank connected through the night.

===motor-start|motor-start|Motor Starting Voltage Dip Calculator|Motor Starting Voltage Dip Calculator|Estimate bus voltage dip when a large motor starts across the line, using locked-rotor kVA against source short-circuit kVA. Free, no signup.
--intro
A motor starting across the line draws locked-rotor current — commonly six times full load — and that inrush pulls the bus voltage down for as long as it takes to accelerate. If the dip is deep enough, contactors drop out, drives fault on undervoltage, and lighting visibly flickers. The screening question is simple: how stiff is the source relative to the motor's starting demand?
--formula
%Voltage dip = LRkVA / (LRkVA + SCkVA) &times; 100
--vars
LRkVA :: Locked-rotor kVA of the motor being started
SCkVA :: Short-circuit kVA available at the bus, a measure of source stiffness
--example
A motor with 1,200 kVA locked-rotor demand on a bus with 20,000 kVA short-circuit capacity:
Dip = 1200 / (1200 + 20000) &times; 100 = 5.7%
Acceptable for most equipment. Halve the source stiffness to 10,000 kVA and the dip becomes 10.7%, which is where contactor dropout and drive undervoltage trips start to appear.
--standard
The screening method is documented in IEEE Std 399 (the Brown Book). Motor locked-rotor codes are defined by NEMA MG 1, and the code letter on the nameplate is what gives you locked-rotor kVA per horsepower.
--limits
A steady-state screening estimate. It does not model the acceleration curve, the load torque the motor is starting against, or how long the dip persists — and duration matters as much as depth for whether a contactor actually drops out. Reduced-voltage starting and soft starters change the picture entirely.
--mistakes
Using full-load kVA instead of locked-rotor kVA, which understates the dip by a factor of about six. Ignoring dip duration. Assuming a generator-backed bus behaves like a utility source, when a generator is far softer and dips much further.

===inrush|inrush|Transformer Inrush Current Calculator|Transformer Inrush Current Calculator|Estimate magnetizing inrush current on transformer energisation as a multiple of full-load amps, for upstream breaker and fuse selection. Free, no signup.
--intro
Energising a transformer draws a magnetizing inrush that is far above full-load current — commonly 8 to 12 times, decaying over several cycles. It is not a fault, but an upstream protective device cannot tell the difference unless it has been selected to ride through it. Getting this wrong produces a transformer that trips its own feeder breaker every time it is energised.
--formula
FLA = kVA &times; 1000 / (1.732 &times; V)  [three-phase]
Peak inrush &asymp; M &times; FLA
--vars
kVA :: Transformer nameplate rating
V :: Line-to-line voltage on the side being energised
M :: Inrush multiplier, typically 8 to 12 for modern units
--example
A 1000 kVA transformer energised from a 480 V bus:
FLA = 1000 &times; 1000 / (1.732 &times; 480) = 1,203 A
At a multiplier of 10, peak inrush is roughly 12,000 A for the first cycle.
The upstream device must not see that as a fault, which usually means an instantaneous setting above it or a deliberately delayed curve.
--standard
IEEE C57.12.00 covers transformer requirements. Overcurrent protection limits for transformers are set by NEC Article 450, which permits higher primary protection specifically to allow for inrush.
--limits
The multiplier is an estimate, not a measurement. Actual inrush depends on residual core flux and the exact point on the voltage wave at which the switch closes, and worst-case energisation can exceed the typical figure. Magnitude also decays over several cycles, so a single peak number does not describe the whole event.
--mistakes
Setting instantaneous protection from full-load current alone. Assuming inrush is the same on every energisation when it depends on residual flux from the last de-energisation. Applying a low multiplier to a modern low-loss core, which tends to have higher inrush than older designs.
--related

===grounding|grounding|Ground Rod Resistance Calculator|Ground Rod Resistance Calculator (Dwight's Formula)|Estimate resistance-to-earth of a driven ground rod from soil resistivity, rod length and diameter, using Dwight's formula. Free, no signup.
--intro
The resistance of a driven rod to earth is dominated by soil resistivity and by rod length — and much less than people expect by rod diameter. Doubling length roughly halves resistance; doubling diameter changes it by only a few percent. That is why grounding designs add rods and depth rather than thicker rods, and why the NEC's 25-ohm threshold is met with a second electrode rather than a bigger one.
--formula
R = [&rho; / (2&pi;L)] &times; [ln(4L/d) &minus; 1]
--vars
&rho; :: Soil resistivity in ohm-metres, which varies enormously: wet clay is tens, dry sand or rock is thousands
L :: Rod length in metres
d :: Rod diameter in metres
R :: Resistance to earth in ohms
--example
A 3 m rod, 16 mm diameter, in 100 ohm-metre soil:
R = [100 / (2&pi; &times; 3)] &times; [ln(4 &times; 3 / 0.016) &minus; 1]
R = 5.31 &times; (6.62 &minus; 1) = 29.8 ohms
Above the 25-ohm figure, so a second electrode is required. Doubling the rod to 6 m brings it to roughly 17 ohms; doubling the diameter instead would barely move it.
--standard
NEC 250.53(A)(2) requires a supplemental electrode where a single rod exceeds 25 ohms. IEEE Std 81 covers measurement of earth resistivity and ground resistance; IEEE Std 80 covers substation grounding design.
--limits
Dwight's formula assumes uniform soil, which real ground is not — resistivity varies with depth, moisture and season, and a summer measurement can be far better than the same rod in a dry autumn. It models one rod: multiple rods interact, and their combined resistance is higher than simple parallel arithmetic suggests unless they are spaced at least one rod length apart.
--mistakes
Treating a calculation as a substitute for measurement, when the code requirement is about measured resistance. Specifying a larger diameter to hit a resistance target. Spacing multiple rods too closely, so their shells of influence overlap and the array performs worse than expected.

===amps-to-kw|amps-to-kw|Amps to kW Calculator|Amps to kW Calculator (1-Phase, 3-Phase, DC)|Convert amps to kilowatts for single-phase, three-phase and DC circuits, with the power factor term that makes the difference. Free, no signup.
--intro
Amps measure current. Kilowatts measure real power &mdash; the part actually doing work. Converting between them needs the voltage, and on an AC circuit it also needs the power factor, because current and voltage are not in step. Leave power factor out and the answer comes back high by however far the load sits from unity, which on a motor-heavy feeder is 10 to 20 percent.
The three-phase case carries an extra factor of the square root of three. That term is not a fudge: it falls out of three voltages 120 degrees apart, and it is the single most commonly dropped part of this calculation.
--formula
kW = (V &times; I &times; PF) / 1000  [single-phase]
kW = (1.732 &times; V &times; I &times; PF) / 1000  [three-phase]
kW = (V &times; I) / 1000  [DC]
--vars
V :: Voltage. Line-to-line for three-phase, line-to-neutral for single-phase
I :: Current in amperes
PF :: Power factor, between 0 and 1. Unity on a purely resistive load; roughly 0.8 to 0.9 on a motor
1.732 :: The square root of three, which appears in every three-phase power calculation
--example
A 100 A three-phase load at 480 V with a power factor of 0.9:
kW = (1.732 &times; 480 &times; 100 &times; 0.9) / 1000 = 74.8 kW
The same 100 A single-phase at 240 V, purely resistive:
kW = (240 &times; 100 &times; 1.0) / 1000 = 24 kW
Same current, very different power. The voltage and the number of phases do most of the work here.
--standard
There is no code section governing the arithmetic; it is the definition of real power. The place a standard enters is the current you put in: NEC Article 220 covers how load current is calculated for sizing purposes, and NEC 430.250 gives the full-load current tables for motors rather than letting you derive them.
--limits
This returns real power at the operating point you describe. It is not a demand figure, and it says nothing about what happens while a motor starts, where current can run six to eight times full-load for a few seconds. On a non-linear load &mdash; drives, rectifiers, switch-mode supplies &mdash; a single power factor also hides the distortion component, and the true power factor is lower than the displacement figure a meter reports.
--mistakes
Dropping the square root of three on a three-phase circuit, which overstates power by 73 percent. Using unity power factor because the real figure is unknown, which overstates it again. Mixing line-to-line and line-to-neutral voltage on the same calculation. Reading nameplate amps as operating amps: a nameplate states full-load current, and most equipment does not run there.

===kw-to-amps|kw-to-amps|kW to Amps Calculator|kW to Amps Calculator (1-Phase, 3-Phase, DC)|Convert kilowatts to amps for single-phase, three-phase and DC circuits, and get the current a conductor actually has to carry. Free, no signup.
--intro
This is the direction that matters for sizing. Equipment is specified in kilowatts and conductors are sized in amperes, so every feeder calculation starts by turning one into the other. The current you get out is the figure that drives conductor ampacity, overcurrent device rating and voltage drop.
Power factor works the opposite way here: a lower power factor means more current for the same kilowatts, not less. That is the whole reason power factor correction pays for itself &mdash; the real power is unchanged, but the current the system carries falls.
--formula
I = (kW &times; 1000) / (V &times; PF)  [single-phase]
I = (kW &times; 1000) / (1.732 &times; V &times; PF)  [three-phase]
I = (kW &times; 1000) / V  [DC]
--vars
kW :: Real power
V :: Voltage. Line-to-line for three-phase
PF :: Power factor, between 0 and 1
I :: Resulting current in amperes
--example
A 75 kW three-phase load at 480 V, power factor 0.9:
I = (75 &times; 1000) / (1.732 &times; 480 &times; 0.9) = 100.2 A
Drop the power factor to 0.75 and the same 75 kW draws 120.2 A &mdash; twenty percent more current for identical work, which can be the difference between one conductor size and the next.
--standard
The arithmetic is definitional. What the result feeds into is not: NEC 210.19 and 215.2 govern conductor sizing for branch circuits and feeders, NEC 310.16 gives the ampacity tables, and NEC 210.20 and 215.3 set how the overcurrent device relates to the calculated load. For continuous loads, the 125 percent rule in NEC 210.19(A) applies on top of this figure.

Sizing a conductor on this number alone, with no continuous-load factor and no derating for ambient temperature or conduit fill. Forgetting that the overcurrent device, not the calculated current, is what the conductor must coordinate with. Using the same power factor for a resistive and a motor load. --intro

The useful thing about this conversion is what it does not need: power factor. kVA is already apparent power, the product of volts and amps, so the power factor term that belongs in every kW calculation has no place here. V :: Voltage on the side you are calculating. Line-to-line for three-phase

The number that matters for sizing is input power, which is higher, because the motor is not perfectly efficient. A 50 hp motor does not draw 37 kW; it draws 37 kW divided by its efficiency. Converters that stop at the 0.7457 multiplier give you the shaft figure and let you under-size the supply.

kW (shaft output) = HP &times; 0.7457

HP :: Nameplate horsepower, mechanical output
0.7457 :: Watts per horsepower, divided by a thousand. 1 hp = 745.7 W
&eta; :: Efficiency as a decimal. Commonly 0.90 to 0.96 on a modern premium-efficiency motor
PF :: Power factor at full load, typically 0.80 to 0.88 on an induction motor

A 50 hp motor at 460 V three-phase, 93 percent efficient, power factor 0.86:

This covers steady-state operation at full load. It says nothing about starting: locked-rotor current typically runs six to eight times full-load amps, which is what sizes a soft starter or sets the voltage dip on the bus. Efficiency and power factor both fall at part load, so a motor running at 40 percent load draws proportionally more current than this suggests.

===wire-size|ampacity|Wire Size Calculator|Wire Size Calculator (What Size Wire for X Amps)|Size a conductor properly: the 125% continuous-load factor, the termination temperature limit under NEC 110.14(C), and ambient and bundling adjustment. Free, no signup.
--intro
"What size wire for 100 amps" has no single answer, which is why the charts that give one are so often wrong. The conductor has to satisfy two separate checks, and the answer is whichever is larger.
The first is the termination temperature. Equipment terminals are listed for 60&deg;C or 75&deg;C, and NEC 110.14(C) says the whole circuit is limited by the lowest-rated termination in it &mdash; so a 90&deg;C conductor landing on a 75&deg;C lug is sized from the 75&deg;C column no matter what the insulation can take. The second is the conductor's own derating: ambient temperature and the number of current-carrying conductors bundled together both reduce what it can safely carry, and that calculation runs on the insulation's own column.
--formula
Required ampacity = (continuous load &times; 1.25) + non-continuous load
Derated ampacity = base ampacity at the insulation rating &times; ambient factor &times; bundling factor
Conductor = the larger of the two results
--vars
Continuous load :: Any load expected to run three hours or more, per NEC 210.19(A)
Termination rating :: 60 or 75&deg;C, from the equipment listing, per NEC 110.14(C)
Ambient factor :: From NEC 310.15(B)(1), applied when ambient is not 30&deg;C
Bundling factor :: From NEC 310.15(C)(1), applied for four or more current-carrying conductors
--example
A 100 A continuous load, copper, 75&deg;C terminations, in a 40&deg;C attic with six current-carrying conductors in the raceway:
Termination check: 100 &times; 1.25 = 125 A required. In the 75&deg;C copper column, 1 AWG carries 130 A. 1 AWG passes.
Derating check: 1 AWG copper at 90&deg;C is 145 A. At 40&deg;C ambient the factor is 0.91, and six conductors gives 0.80: 145 &times; 0.91 &times; 0.80 = 105.6 A, against an actual load of 100 A. 1 AWG passes again.
Both checks land on 1 AWG, so that is the answer. Change the ambient to 50&deg;C and the derating check gives 145 &times; 0.82 &times; 0.80 = 95.1 A, below the load, and the conductor goes up to 1/0 while the termination check has not moved at all.
--standard
NEC 110.14(C) sets the termination temperature limitation. NEC 210.19(A) and 215.2(A) give the 125 percent continuous-load factor for branch circuits and feeders. Base ampacities are in NEC Table 310.16, ambient correction in 310.15(B)(1) and bundling adjustment in 310.15(C)(1). Overcurrent device selection follows NEC 240.4 and the standard ratings in 240.6.
--limits
This sizes for ampacity only. It does not check voltage drop, which on a long run is frequently what forces the conductor up rather than heat, and it does not check short-circuit withstand. It covers conductors in a raceway or cable at a single ambient; buried runs, conductors in sunlight on a rooftop and conductors passing through different ambients each have their own rules. It also does not handle motor circuits, where NEC Article 430 requires the table full-load current rather than the actual load, or the 83 percent allowance in 310.12 for certain dwelling services.

Sizing from the 90&deg;C column because the wire is THHN, when the terminals are listed for 75&deg;C. Applying the 125 percent factor to the derated figure rather than running the two checks separately. Forgetting that the neutral counts as a current-carrying conductor on a circuit with significant harmonic content. Counting only the conductors of one circuit when several share the raceway. Treating a chart found online as authoritative when it states neither the termination temperature nor the ambient it assumed.

===awg-to-mm2|awg-to-mm2|AWG to mm&sup2; Converter|AWG to mm&sup2; Converter (Wire Size Conversion)|Convert AWG and kcmil to square millimetres and back, with the nearest IEC standard size. Built from the AWG geometric definition. Free, no signup.
--intro
AWG is not an arbitrary list of sizes. It is a geometric progression, and knowing that is the difference between looking a number up and understanding what it means: the diameter of gauge n is 0.005 inch multiplied by 92 raised to the power (36 minus n) over 39. Every size in the system falls out of that one expression.
Two consequences follow immediately. The familiar rules of thumb are exact properties of the definition rather than approximations &mdash; six gauge steps is almost precisely four times the area, three steps almost precisely double. And the gauge numbers run backwards, because they originally counted the number of drawing dies the wire was pulled through, so a larger number means a thinner conductor. Above 4/0 the system runs out and sizes are given in thousands of circular mils instead.
--formula
Diameter (in) = 0.005 &times; 92<sup>(36 &minus; n)/39</sup>, where n is the gauge number and 4/0 is n = &minus;3
Circular mils = (diameter in mils)<sup>2</sup>
Area (mm&sup2;) = circular mils &times; 0.0005067
--vars
n :: Gauge number. 1/0 is 0, 2/0 is &minus;1, 3/0 is &minus;2, 4/0 is &minus;3
Circular mil :: The area of a circle one mil (0.001 inch) in diameter
kcmil :: One thousand circular mils, used for every size above 4/0
IEC size :: A standard metric cross-section from the series 1.5, 2.5, 4, 6, 10, 16, 25 mm&sup2; and upward
--example
Take 12 AWG. The gauge number is 12, so the diameter is 0.005 &times; 92<sup>24/39</sup> = 0.0808 inch, which is 80.8 mils.
Squaring that gives 6,530 circular mils, the figure NEC Chapter 9 Table 8 lists for 12 AWG. Multiplying by 0.0005067 gives 3.31 mm&sup2;.
The nearest IEC standard size is 4 mm&sup2;, which is 21 percent larger. There is no metric size that equals 12 AWG, which is the single most important thing this conversion tells you.
Checking the rule of thumb: six steps up from 12 AWG is 6 AWG, at 26,240 circular mils. That is 4.02 times the area of 12 AWG, not exactly four, because the ratio is 92 raised to the power 12 over 39.
--standard
The AWG geometric definition is the basis of the conductor sizes in ASTM B258. NEC Chapter 9 Table 8 gives the standardized conductor properties used for code work in the United States, including circular mil area, cross-sectional area in square millimetres, and DC resistance for both solid and stranded constructions. IEC 60228 defines the metric conductor classes and their standard cross-sections. Ampacity comes from NEC Table 310.16 and its adjustment rules, or from IEC 60364-5-52 outside North America.
--limits
This converts area and nothing else. Treating the nearest metric size as an equivalent when it is usually several percent away, and substituting downward without noticing. Converting an ampacity figure along with the area. Using the conductor area for conduit fill instead of the overall insulated diameter. Assuming the gauge numbers increase with size. Reading a cmil figure as if it were a mm&sup2; figure, which differs by a factor of about 1,974. Specifying a metric conductor for a North American installation without checking that the terminations are listed for it.

===kva-to-kw|kva-to-kw|kVA to kW Converter|kVA to kW Calculator (and kW to kVA)|Convert kVA to kW and back, with reactive power. Why there is no fixed conversion factor, where the 0.8 assumption comes from, and why transformers are rated in kVA. Free, no signup.
--intro
There is no conversion factor between kVA and kW. That is the whole answer, and every wrong result in this calculation comes from wanting one.
kVA is apparent power: volts times amps, the product the equipment in the supply path actually has to carry. kW is real power: the part doing work. The ratio between them is the power factor, and power factor is a property of the load &mdash; what is connected and how it draws current. Nothing about the transformer, the cable or the switchgear determines it.
That is precisely why transformers are rated in kVA. The manufacturer cannot know what you will connect, so it rates the equipment by what it must carry rather than by what the load will usefully do with it. A 500 kVA transformer is a 500 kVA transformer whether the load runs at 0.95 or 0.65.
--formula
kW = kVA &times; PF
kVA = kW / PF
kvar = &radic;(kVA&sup2; &minus; kW&sup2;)
kVA&sup2; = kW&sup2; + kvar&sup2;
--vars
kVA :: Apparent power. What the transformer, conductors and switchgear are sized for
kW :: Real power. The component that does work and that the meter bills for
PF :: Power factor, between 0 and 1. The ratio kW / kVA, set by the load
kvar :: Reactive power. Current the system carries that produces no work
--example
A facility draws 500 kVA at 0.8 power factor.
kW = 500 &times; 0.8 = 400 kW of real power.
kvar = &radic;(500&sup2; &minus; 400&sup2;) = &radic;(250,000 &minus; 160,000) = 300 kvar of reactive power.
So 400 kW of useful output requires 500 kVA of capacity through every component upstream &mdash; 25 percent more apparent power than real power, and the conductors are sized for the 500.
Now correct the power factor to 0.95 without changing the real load. The 400 kW still needs doing, but the apparent power falls to 400 / 0.95 = 421 kVA. The same work now moves through 79 kVA less capacity, which is the entire economic case for power-factor correction.
--standard
This is the definition of apparent, real and reactive power rather than a code rule, and it holds for any single-frequency AC system. Transformer kVA ratings follow IEEE C57.12.00 for liquid-immersed and IEEE C57.12.01 for dry-type units. Generator sets are conventionally rated with a kW output and a 0.8 power factor, giving the alternator kVA, under ISO 8528. Utility power-factor penalties and any required correction are set by the local tariff, not by a standard.
--limits
The relationship assumes a single frequency and reasonably sinusoidal current. Where there is significant harmonic distortion the simple power triangle stops describing the system: distortion adds an apparent-power component that is neither real nor conventionally reactive, so the measured power factor splits into a displacement part and a distortion part and kW is no longer kVA times the displacement power factor. It also says nothing about which direction the reactive power flows &mdash; leading and lagging power factor have the same magnitude here &mdash; and nothing about whether a power factor is acceptable, which is a tariff question. DC has no power factor at all, so kVA is not a meaningful quantity on a DC system.
--mistakes
Looking for a fixed conversion factor, or assuming 0.8 because generator sets are rated that way. Converting a transformer's kVA nameplate into kW and treating the result as a capacity limit, when the limit is the kVA. Sizing conductors or switchgear from kW rather than kVA. Using the simple triangle on a heavily non-linear load, where distortion breaks it. Confusing kvar with kVA. Assuming correcting power factor reduces the real energy bill, when what it reduces is apparent power, demand charges and any penalty. Treating a leading power factor as equivalent to the same figure lagging.

===three-phase-power|three-phase-power|Three-Phase Power Calculator|Three-Phase Power Calculator (Line and Phase Values)|Solve for voltage, current, power or power factor in a balanced three-phase system, with line and phase values for delta and wye. Free, no signup.
--intro
Almost every three-phase mistake is a factor of &radic;3 in the wrong place, and the reason is that the formula people memorise hides where it belongs.
Power in a balanced three-phase system is &radic;3 &times; line voltage &times; line current &times; power factor. The &radic;3 is there because line voltage and phase voltage are not the same thing, and which one differs depends on how the windings are connected. In a wye, line voltage is &radic;3 times phase voltage and the line current passes straight through one winding, so line and phase current are equal. In a delta it reverses: each winding sees full line voltage, while line current splits between two windings, so line current is &radic;3 times winding current.
Nameplates and meters give line values. The phase values are what you need when you are working inside a winding, checking one leg, or reading a current transformer.
--formula
kW = &radic;3 &times; V<sub>line</sub> &times; I<sub>line</sub> &times; PF / 1000
kVA = &radic;3 &times; V<sub>line</sub> &times; I<sub>line</sub> / 1000
Wye: V<sub>phase</sub> = V<sub>line</sub> / &radic;3, I<sub>phase</sub> = I<sub>line</sub>
Delta: V<sub>phase</sub> = V<sub>line</sub>, I<sub>phase</sub> = I<sub>line</sub> / &radic;3
--vars
V line :: Line-to-line voltage. The figure on the nameplate and on the drawing
I line :: Current in one of the three supply conductors
PF :: Power factor of the load, between 0 and 1
&radic;3 :: 1.732, from the 120-degree phase displacement between the three voltages
--example
A motor on a 480 V three-phase supply draws 100 A at 0.85 power factor.
kVA = 1.732 &times; 480 &times; 100 / 1000 = 83.1 kVA.
kW = 83.1 &times; 0.85 = 70.6 kW of real power, with 43.8 kvar reactive.
Now the part that gets missed. If that load is wye-connected, each winding sees 480 / 1.732 = 277 V and carries the full 100 A. If it is delta-connected, each winding sees the full 480 V and carries 100 / 1.732 = 57.7 A. The supply is identical in both cases and the line measurements are identical; only the internal stresses differ.
--standard
This is circuit theory rather than a code requirement, and it holds for any balanced three-phase system at a single frequency. The voltage designations used in North America, such as 208Y/120, 480Y/277 and 240 delta, come from ANSI C84.1, which also sets the permitted voltage ranges at the service and at the point of utilization. Conductor sizing from the resulting current follows NEC 310 and, for motors specifically, Article 430, which requires the table full-load current rather than measured load.
--limits
Everything here assumes a balanced load, meaning equal current in all three phases. On an unbalanced system the single-figure answer stops being meaningful: real power has to be summed per phase, and a neutral current appears that the balanced formula does not predict. It also assumes reasonably sinusoidal current, so a heavily non-linear load breaks the simple power-factor relationship, and it says nothing about which direction reactive power flows. The connection shown is the load's, which is not necessarily the transformer secondary's.
--mistakes
Applying &radic;3 to a single-phase circuit, or omitting it from a three-phase one. Using phase voltage where the formula wants line voltage, which on a 480Y/277 system gives an answer low by a factor of 1.732. Dividing three-phase power by three to get per-phase power and then applying &radic;3 again. Treating a measured line current as the winding current in a delta load. Using the balanced formula on a visibly unbalanced panel. Reading kVA as kW, which only match at unity power factor.

A 100 kW IT load is a 100 kW thermal load, so the problem reduces to a unit conversion rather than an energy balance.
--formula
Tons = BTU/h / 12,000
kW (thermal) = tons &times; 3.517

Equipment capacity ratings and the conditions they are measured at come from AHRI certification standards, which matters because a nominal tonnage is stated at specific entering and ambient conditions and real capacity falls away from them. This converts capacity units and nothing else. It does not size a cooling system: it ignores the latent load from humidity control, the outside-air and envelope loads in a mixed-use space, the power the cooling plant itself draws, and the redundancy the facility is designed to, all of which sit between a heat load and an equipment schedule. Nominal equipment tonnage is rated at standard conditions and the delivered capacity differs at real entering water and ambient air temperatures. The IT assumption that electrical input equals thermal output is very close but not exact, and it does not hold for loads that do external work, such as motors driving something outside the space.

Reading a ton as a weight or as a size of unit rather than a rate of heat removal. Confusing cooling capacity in kW with the electrical power the chiller draws, which differ by the system's efficiency. Sizing cooling from a UPS or transformer rating rather than the actual IT load, which inflates the figure by whatever the equipment is oversized by. Taking nominal tonnage as available capacity at design-day conditions. Adding spare capacity and redundancy as if they were the same thing. Applying the electrical-equals-thermal shortcut to motor loads doing work outside the room.

--intro
<b>Ohm's law says voltage equals current times resistance: V = I &times; R.</b> Add the power relation, P = V &times; I, and any two of the four quantities fix the other two. That is all this calculator does: give it two, and it returns the missing pair and tells you which form of the equations it used.
It is the right tool for a resistive load on DC, and for a resistive load on AC such as a heater or an incandescent lamp. It is the wrong tool for anything with a motor, a transformer or a power supply in it, where current and voltage are out of step and power factor has to be included.

R :: Resistance of the load, in ohms
P :: Power dissipated in the load, in watts

===ah-to-kwh|ah-to-kwh|Ah to kWh Calculator|Ah to kWh Calculator (Amp-Hours to kWh)|Convert battery amp-hours to kilowatt-hours and back at any nominal voltage, and see why Ah figures cannot be compared across voltages. Free, no signup.
--intro
<b>Kilowatt-hours are amp-hours times nominal voltage, divided by a thousand.</b> Amp-hours measure charge and kilowatt-hours measure energy, so there is no converting one to the other without the voltage. A 100 Ah battery is 1.2 kWh at 12 V and 4.8 kWh at 48 V.
This is why cells and small batteries are quoted in amp-hours while storage systems are quoted in kWh or MWh. Energy is the figure that can be compared between systems at different voltages; amp-hours cannot.
--formula
kWh = Ah &times; V / 1000
Ah = kWh &times; 1000 / V
--vars
Ah :: Rated capacity in amp-hours
V :: Nominal voltage of the cell, module or string, in volts
kWh :: Nameplate energy in kilowatt-hours
--example
A 100 Ah battery at 48 V nominal:
kWh = 100 &times; 48 / 1000 = 4.8 kWh
A single 280 Ah lithium iron phosphate cell at 3.2 V nominal:
kWh = 280 &times; 3.2 / 1000 = 0.896 kWh
Going the other way, 10 kWh at 48 V is 10 &times; 1000 / 48 = 208.3 Ah.
--standard
The conversion is definitional. How the amp-hour rating itself is established is not: it depends on the discharge rate, end voltage and temperature the manufacturer tested at, which for stationary lead-acid batteries follows practices such as IEEE 485 and for lithium-ion cells is stated on the cell data sheet.
--limits
The result is nameplate energy at nominal voltage. A battery's voltage falls as it discharges, so the energy actually delivered differs somewhat from this figure. It is also not usable energy: depth-of-discharge limits, conversion losses and capacity fade all reduce what a system can deliver, which is what the BESS sizing calculator accounts for.
--mistakes
Comparing amp-hour ratings of batteries at different voltages. Using charging voltage or full-charge voltage in place of nominal voltage. Treating nameplate kWh as usable kWh. Adding the amp-hours of cells wired in series, where the voltage adds and the amp-hours do not.

===battery-runtime|battery-runtime|Battery Runtime Calculator|Battery Runtime Calculator (UPS &amp; Backup)|Estimate how long a battery will carry a load from its amp-hours, voltage, usable depth of discharge and inverter efficiency. Free, no signup.
--intro
<b>Runtime is usable battery energy divided by the load.</b> Usable energy is amp-hours times voltage, reduced by how deeply the battery may be discharged and by what the inverter loses on the way to the load. A 100 Ah, 48 V battery holds 4,800 Wh on the nameplate and delivers noticeably less than that to an AC load.
The estimate is dependable when the discharge is slow, over several hours. It becomes optimistic as the discharge gets faster, and for a UPS that empties its battery in ten or fifteen minutes it should not be used at all. The calculator flags the discharge rate so you can tell which case you are in.
--formula
Usable energy (Wh) = Ah &times; V &times; depth of discharge &times; efficiency
Runtime (h) = usable energy / load (W)
--vars
Ah :: Rated capacity of the battery in amp-hours
V :: Nominal battery voltage
Depth of discharge :: The fraction of capacity that may be used, as a percentage
Efficiency :: Inverter and wiring efficiency between battery and load, as a percentage. Use 100 for a DC load fed directly
Load :: Power drawn by the load, in watts
--example
A 100 Ah, 48 V battery, discharged to 80 percent depth through a 90 percent efficient inverter, carrying 1,000 W:
Usable energy = 100 &times; 48 &times; 0.80 &times; 0.90 = 3,456 Wh
Runtime = 3,456 / 1,000 = 3.46 hours, or about 3 h 27 min
The load is 1,000 W on a 4,800 Wh battery, a little over 0.2C. Raise the load to 4,800 W and the arithmetic says 43 minutes, but a lead-acid battery discharged at 1C delivers far less than its rated amp-hours, so the real figure would be shorter.
--standard
For stationary lead-acid batteries, IEEE 485 is the recommended practice for sizing against a duty cycle, and it works from the manufacturer's discharge tables with temperature, aging and design margins applied. IEEE 1184 covers battery selection for UPS systems. A calculation of this kind is the first estimate that comes before either.
--limits
Rated amp-hours apply at one discharge rate, commonly the 8, 10 or 20 hour rate for lead-acid. Discharged faster, a lead-acid battery gives up less charge, which this method ignores. It also leaves out temperature, the capacity lost with age, and the end-of-discharge voltage the equipment needs. UPS batteries are specified in watts per cell at a given runtime for exactly this reason, and a UPS runtime should come from those tables.
--mistakes
Using nameplate energy with no allowance for depth of discharge or inverter losses. Applying a slow-rate amp-hour rating to a fast discharge. Sizing for a new battery and forgetting it has to meet the runtime at end of life. Confusing the load in watts with the UPS rating in VA.

===pue|pue|PUE Calculator|PUE Calculator (Power Usage Effectiveness)|Calculate data center PUE and DCiE from total facility power and IT power, with the overhead in kW and what it costs over a year. Free, no signup.
--intro
<b>PUE is total facility power divided by IT equipment power.</b> A PUE of 1.5 means that for every kilowatt delivered to servers, storage and network equipment, another half kilowatt goes to cooling, power conversion losses, lighting and everything else in the building. A PUE of 1.0 would mean no overhead at all, which no real facility reaches.
The number is only as good as the two measurements behind it. Both have to cover the same period, the total has to be taken at the utility meter or its equivalent, and the IT figure should be taken as close to the equipment as the metering allows.
--formula
PUE = total facility power / IT equipment power
DCiE = 1 / PUE, as a percentage
Overhead = total facility power &minus; IT equipment power
--vars
Total facility power :: Everything the data center draws, including cooling, UPS and distribution losses and lighting, in kW
IT equipment power :: Power delivered to servers, storage and network equipment, in kW
DCiE :: Data center infrastructure efficiency, the reciprocal of PUE
--example
A facility draws 1,500 kW in total and delivers 1,000 kW to IT equipment:
PUE = 1,500 / 1,000 = 1.50, and DCiE = 66.7%
Overhead = 1,500 &minus; 1,000 = 500 kW
Held for a year, that overhead is 500 &times; 8,760 = 4,380,000 kWh. At $0.08 per kWh it costs about $350,400. Bringing the same IT load to a PUE of 1.3 would cut the overhead to 300 kW and save 1,752,000 kWh a year.
--standard
PUE was introduced by The Green Grid and is standardized as ISO/IEC 30134-2, which defines the measurement points and requires the figure to be based on energy over a full year. A PUE taken from instantaneous power, as this calculator does, is a snapshot and should be labelled as one.
--limits
A snapshot PUE moves with the weather and with IT load, so a single reading can be well away from the annual figure. PUE also says nothing about how efficiently the IT equipment uses the power it receives, and it can improve simply because IT load went up while overhead stayed the same. It does not account for water use or for the carbon intensity of the supply.
--mistakes
Comparing a design PUE at full load with a measured PUE at partial load. Measuring IT power at the UPS output and so counting distribution losses as IT load. Quoting the best winter reading as the facility's PUE. Comparing PUE between sites in different climates as though it measured the quality of the design alone.

===wire-resistance|wire-resistance|Wire Resistance Calculator|Wire Resistance Calculator (Copper &amp; Aluminum)|Find the resistance of a copper or aluminum conductor from its size and length, one way or out and back, in ohms and ohms per 1000 ft. Free, no signup.
--intro
<b>A conductor's resistance is its length divided by its cross-sectional area, times a constant for the metal.</b> Double the length and resistance doubles. Go up three AWG sizes and the area doubles, so resistance halves. Aluminum has about 1.6 times the resistance of copper at the same size.
Resistance is what voltage drop and conductor heating come from, so this is the figure underneath both. For a two-wire circuit the current flows out and back, and the resistance that matters is that of both conductors, twice the one-way figure.
--formula
R = K &times; L / A
--vars
R :: Resistance in ohms
K :: 12.9 for copper, 21.2 for aluminum, in ohm-circular mils per foot at about 75&deg;C
L :: Length in feet. One way, or doubled for a two-wire circuit
A :: Conductor area in circular mils. 12 AWG is 6,530; 4/0 AWG is 211,600
--example
100 ft of 12 AWG copper, one way:
R = 12.9 &times; 100 / 6,530 = 0.1975 &Omega;
That is 1.975 &Omega; per 1000 ft, in line with the figures NEC Chapter 9, Table 8 gives for 12 AWG copper.
For a two-wire circuit the current passes through 200 ft of conductor, so the resistance is 0.3951 &Omega;. At 16 A that drops 16 &times; 0.3951 = 6.3 V, which on a 120 V circuit is 5.3 percent, well past the 3 percent usually recommended for a branch circuit.
--standard
NEC Chapter 9, Table 8 gives DC resistance for conductors at 75&deg;C, and Table 9 gives AC resistance and reactance for conductors in conduit. Conductor areas in circular mils follow the AWG definition in ASTM B258. The K-factor method used here is an approximation to Table 8.
--limits
This is DC resistance at one temperature. Resistance rises with temperature, by roughly 0.4 percent per degree Celsius for copper and aluminum, so a conductor running cooler than 75&deg;C has less. On AC, skin effect and the conduit material raise the effective resistance of large conductors above the DC figure, and reactance adds to the impedance; for sizes above about 4/0, use NEC Table 9. It does not include the resistance of terminations and splices.
--mistakes
Using the one-way length for a two-wire circuit. Using copper figures for aluminum conductors. Expecting resistance to halve with each AWG size, when it takes three sizes. Ignoring temperature when comparing a calculated figure with a measurement taken on a cold cable.

===dc-ac-ratio|dc-ac-ratio|DC/AC Ratio Calculator|DC/AC Ratio Calculator (Inverter Loading Ratio)|Calculate a solar plant's DC/AC ratio from array DC capacity and inverter AC rating, and see how much the inverter would clip at full output. Free, no signup.
--intro
<b>The DC/AC ratio is the array's DC nameplate capacity divided by the inverter's AC rating.</b> It is also called the inverter loading ratio. A 130 kW array on a 100 kW inverter has a ratio of 1.30.
A ratio above 1.0 is deliberate. An array reaches its nameplate output only under standard test conditions, which it rarely sees in the field: modules run hot, the sun is seldom square to them, and wiring and soiling take their share. Oversizing the array fills more of the inverter's capacity for more hours of the day. The price is clipping, where the inverter caps its output during the hours the array could produce more.
--formula
DC/AC ratio = array DC capacity (kWp) / inverter AC rating (kW)
Array DC capacity = number of modules &times; module rating (W) / 1000
--vars
Array DC capacity :: Sum of module nameplate ratings at standard test conditions, in kWp
Inverter AC rating :: Rated continuous AC output of the inverter, in kW
--example
A string inverter rated 100 kW AC is fed by 240 modules of 540 W each:
Array DC capacity = 240 &times; 540 / 1000 = 129.6 kWp
DC/AC ratio = 129.6 / 100 = 1.30
If the array ever did reach nameplate, it would offer the inverter 30 percent more than it can pass, and the excess would be clipped. In practice that happens on a limited number of cool, clear hours, which is why a ratio in the region of 1.2 to 1.3 is a common design choice.
--standard
The ratio itself is a design figure, not a code limit. The limits that bound it are electrical: NEC 690.7 sets the maximum DC voltage, NEC 690.8 the circuit currents, and the inverter data sheet gives the maximum DC power and short-circuit current its input will accept. An array can satisfy the ratio a developer wants and still exceed one of those.
--limits
The ratio does not tell you how much energy is clipped over a year. That depends on the site's irradiance, temperature, tilt and tracking, and it takes an hourly production model to estimate. It is also unaffected by module degradation, which lowers the effective ratio as the plant ages, and it says nothing about whether the string voltage and current fit the inverter's input window.
--mistakes
Dividing AC by DC and reporting the inverse. Using the interconnection limit in place of the inverter rating without saying so. Treating the clipping percentage at nameplate as the annual energy loss, which is far smaller. Choosing a ratio from a rule of thumb for a different climate or a different mounting system.

===airflow-cfm|airflow-cfm|Airflow (CFM) Calculator|Airflow CFM Calculator (Heat Load to CFM)|Find the airflow needed to remove a heat load: kW and air temperature rise to CFM, with CFM per kW for server and rack cooling. Free, no signup.
--intro
<b>The airflow needed to carry away a heat load is the load in BTU per hour divided by 1.08 times the air temperature rise in degrees Fahrenheit.</b> For 10 kW of IT equipment and a 20&deg;F rise that is about 1,580 CFM, or 158 CFM per kW. Halve the temperature rise and the airflow doubles.
This is the sum behind every question about whether a rack, a row or a room has enough air. Almost all the electrical power going into IT equipment leaves as heat, so the heat load in kW is the electrical load in kW.
--viz
cooling
--formula
CFM = BTU/h / (1.08 &times; &Delta;T)
BTU/h = kW &times; 3,412
--vars
CFM :: Airflow in cubic feet per minute
&Delta;T :: Temperature rise of the air across the equipment, in &deg;F
1.08 :: A constant for standard air: density 0.075 lb/ft&sup3; &times; specific heat 0.24 BTU/lb&middot;&deg;F &times; 60 minutes
kW :: Sensible heat load, which for IT equipment is its electrical input
--example
A rack drawing 10 kW, with air entering at 75&deg;F and leaving at 95&deg;F:
BTU/h = 10 &times; 3,412 = 34,121
CFM = 34,121 / (1.08 &times; 20) = 1,580 CFM, or 158 CFM per kW
The same rack with a 30&deg;F rise needs 1,053 CFM. Wider temperature rise is the reason high-density equipment can be cooled with less air than its power suggests, and it is also why hot-aisle temperatures have climbed.
--standard
The equation is the sensible heat equation from the ASHRAE Handbook of Fundamentals. Inlet temperature limits for IT equipment come from the ASHRAE TC 9.9 thermal guidelines, which recommend 64 to 81&deg;F (18 to 27&deg;C) at the equipment inlet for the common air-cooled classes.
--limits
The 1.08 constant is for standard air at sea level. Air is thinner at altitude, so the same heat needs more volume; at 5,000 ft the constant is closer to 0.90. The calculation covers sensible heat only, and it gives the airflow the equipment needs, not what the cooling units must deliver. Bypass and recirculation mean the room supply has to be higher than the sum of the equipment airflows unless containment is good.
--mistakes
Using the room temperature difference in place of the rise across the equipment. Forgetting altitude. Assuming the cooling units only need to supply the equipment airflow, with no allowance for air that bypasses the racks. Treating a nameplate power figure as the heat load, when measured draw is usually well below it.

--intro
<b>Chilled water flow in GPM is the load in BTU per hour divided by 500 times the temperature difference in degrees Fahrenheit.</b> With the load in tons it shortens to GPM = tons &times; 24 / &Delta;T. At the traditional 10&deg;F difference that is 2.4 GPM per ton; at 12&deg;F it is 2.0 and at 16&deg;F it is 1.5.
The temperature difference is the lever. A wider one moves the same heat with less water, which means smaller pipes and less pump energy. It is why chilled water systems for data centers are designed with wider differences than the old comfort-cooling rule of thumb.
--viz
chiller
--formula
GPM = BTU/h / (500 &times; &Delta;T)
GPM = tons &times; 24 / &Delta;T
--vars
GPM :: Water flow in US gallons per minute
&Delta;T :: Difference between return and supply water temperature, in &deg;F
500 :: A constant for water: 8.33 lb/gal &times; 60 minutes &times; specific heat 1.0 BTU/lb&middot;&deg;F
tons :: Cooling load in tons of refrigeration, 12,000 BTU/h each
--example
A 100 ton load with water supplied at 44&deg;F and returning at 54&deg;F:
GPM = 100 &times; 24 / 10 = 240 GPM
Widen the difference to 16&deg;F and the same load needs 150 GPM. Because pump power falls much faster than flow, the saving in pumping energy is larger than the 38 percent cut in flow suggests.
A 500 kW IT load is 500 &times; 3,412 = 1,706,000 BTU/h, or 142 tons. At a 12&deg;F difference it needs 284 GPM.
--standard
The equation is the standard hydronic heat-transfer relation from the ASHRAE Handbook. Chiller ratings are certified under AHRI Standard 550/590, which is where the traditional 44&deg;F leaving water and 2.4 GPM per ton rating condition comes from.
--limits
The constant of 500 is for plain water at typical chilled water temperatures. A glycol mix has a lower specific heat and a higher density, so it needs more flow for the same load: roughly 5 to 15 percent more depending on the type and concentration. The result is the flow at the design load. It does not tell you the temperature difference the coils will actually achieve, and a system that returns water colder than designed needs more flow than this.

Using 2.4 GPM per ton on a system designed for a wider temperature difference. Applying the water constant to a glycol system. ===pump-power|pump-power|Pump Power Calculator|Pump Power Calculator (GPM and Head to HP)|Calculate pump brake horsepower and motor kW from flow in GPM, head in feet and efficiency, and pick the next standard motor size. Free, no signup.
--intro
<b>Pump power is flow times head divided by 3,960, divided again by the pump's efficiency.</b> Flow in GPM times head in feet over 3,960 gives the power delivered to the water. Dividing by pump efficiency gives the brake horsepower the shaft has to supply, and dividing that by motor efficiency gives the electrical input.
Head is the pressure the pump adds, expressed as the height of a column of the liquid. It is not the height the water is lifted, unless the system is open. In a closed chilled water loop the static heights cancel and the head is all friction: pipe, fittings, valves, the coil and the chiller.
--viz
pump
--formula
Water hp = GPM &times; head (ft) &times; SG / 3,960
Brake hp = water hp / pump efficiency
Input kW = brake hp &times; 0.7457 / motor efficiency
--vars
GPM :: Flow in US gallons per minute
Head :: Total dynamic head in feet of liquid
SG :: Specific gravity. 1.0 for water
Pump efficiency :: From the pump curve at the duty point, as a fraction
Motor efficiency :: From the motor nameplate, as a fraction
--example
A chilled water pump delivering 500 GPM against 80 ft of head, with a pump efficiency of 75 percent and a motor efficiency of 93 percent:
Water hp = 500 &times; 80 &times; 1.0 / 3,960 = 10.1 hp
Brake hp = 10.1 / 0.75 = 13.5 hp
Input kW = 13.5 &times; 0.7457 / 0.93 = 10.8 kW
The next standard motor at or above 13.5 hp is 15 hp. Running all year, 10.8 kW is about 94,600 kWh.
--standard
The equations are the standard pump relations published by the Hydraulic Institute and in the ASHRAE Handbook. Motor efficiency classes and standard horsepower ratings come from NEMA MG 1. ASHRAE Standard 90.1 limits pumping power in building hydronic systems and requires variable flow above certain sizes.
--limits
This is one operating point. A pump runs wherever its curve crosses the system curve, and the motor has to be large enough for every point the pump may reach, including runout at low head. The efficiency you enter must be the one at the duty point; away from the best efficiency point it falls quickly. The calculation does not check net positive suction head, which decides whether the pump cavitates.
--mistakes
Using the height of the building as the head on a closed loop. Using the pump's peak efficiency for a duty point far from it. Selecting a motor for the design point with nothing in hand for runout. ===pipe-velocity|pipe-velocity|Pipe Velocity Calculator|Pipe Velocity Calculator (GPM to ft/s)|Calculate water velocity in a pipe from flow in GPM and inside diameter, with a check against common hydronic design limits. Free, no signup.
--intro
<b>Water velocity in feet per second is 0.4085 times the flow in GPM, divided by the square of the pipe's inside diameter in inches.</b> Because diameter is squared, one pipe size makes a large difference: the same 240 GPM runs at 6.0 ft/s in 4 inch pipe and 10.4 ft/s in 3 inch.

--viz
pump
--formula
v = 0.4085 &times; GPM / d&sup2;
--vars
v :: Velocity in feet per second
GPM :: Flow in US gallons per minute
d :: Inside diameter of the pipe in inches, which is not the nominal size
--example
240 GPM in 4 inch Schedule 40 steel pipe, which has an inside diameter of 4.026 in:
v = 0.4085 &times; 240 / 4.026&sup2; = 98.04 / 16.21 = 6.05 ft/s
The same flow in 3 inch Schedule 40, inside diameter 3.068 in, runs at 10.4 ft/s, which is on the fast side for a closed hydronic system.
--standard
The equation is continuity: flow equals velocity times area. Pipe dimensions come from ASME B36.10 for steel pipe. The ASHRAE Handbook gives the customary hydronic sizing limits, commonly quoted as about 4 ft/s in pipe of 2 inches and under and a friction loss of about 4 ft of head per 100 ft in larger pipe.
--limits
Velocity alone does not give pressure drop, which also depends on pipe roughness, fluid viscosity and every fitting in the run. The limits quoted are design conventions for closed hydronic systems and are not requirements; domestic water, condenser water and process piping follow their own. The inside diameter changes with schedule and material, so the same nominal size gives different velocities in steel, copper and plastic.
--mistakes
Entering the nominal pipe size as the diameter. Using Schedule 40 dimensions for Schedule 80 or for copper tube. Judging a pipe on velocity and never checking the pressure drop. Sizing every pipe to the same velocity regardless of diameter, which makes small pipes noisy and large ones needlessly big.

===affinity-laws|affinity-laws|Pump &amp; Fan Affinity Laws Calculator|Affinity Laws Calculator (Pumps and Fans)|Apply the pump and fan affinity laws: enter two speeds and see how flow, head and power change, including the cube-law energy saving. Free, no signup.
--intro
<b>The affinity laws say that flow changes in proportion to speed, head or pressure with the square of speed, and power with the cube.</b> Slow a pump or a fan to 80 percent of its speed and it delivers 80 percent of the flow at 64 percent of the pressure, for 51 percent of the power.
That cube is the whole case for variable speed drives on pumps and fans. A cooling system rarely needs full flow, and trimming the speed by a fifth nearly halves the energy. Throttling a valve or closing a damper to get the same flow wastes most of that saving as pressure drop.
--viz
pump
--formula
Q&#8322; / Q&#8321; = N&#8322; / N&#8321;
H&#8322; / H&#8321; = (N&#8322; / N&#8321;)&sup2;
P&#8322; / P&#8321; = (N&#8322; / N&#8321;)&sup3;
--vars
N :: Rotational speed, in rpm or as a percentage
Q :: Flow, in GPM for a pump or CFM for a fan
H :: Head for a pump, or static pressure for a fan
P :: Shaft power
--example
A pump delivers 500 GPM at 80 ft of head and draws 15 hp at 1,750 rpm. A drive slows it to 1,400 rpm, which is 80 percent speed:
Flow = 500 &times; 0.80 = 400 GPM
Head = 80 &times; 0.80&sup2; = 51.2 ft
Power = 15 &times; 0.80&sup3; = 7.68 hp
The pump moves four fifths of the water for about half the power. At 50 percent speed the power would be one eighth.
--standard
The affinity laws are derived from dimensional analysis of centrifugal machines and appear in the Hydraulic Institute standards for pumps and in AMCA publications for fans, where they are called the fan laws. ASHRAE Standard 90.1 relies on them in requiring variable speed on larger pumps and fans.
--limits
The laws hold for the same machine in a system whose resistance is all friction, so that the operating point slides along a curve through the origin. A system with static head, such as a pump lifting water to a tower or a fan working against a fixed pressure setpoint, does not follow them: flow falls faster than speed, and the power saving is smaller than the cube predicts. They also assume efficiency stays constant, which is reasonable over a moderate speed range and not at very low speeds. Drive and motor losses are extra.
--mistakes
Applying the cube law to a system with significant static head or a fixed pressure setpoint. Forgetting that the same laws make a small overspeed expensive: 110 percent speed takes 133 percent power. Using them to compare two different pumps. Expecting the electrical saving to match the shaft saving exactly, with no allowance for drive losses.

===motor-torque|motor-torque|Motor Torque Calculator|Motor Torque Calculator (HP and RPM to Torque)|Calculate motor torque in lb-ft and N&middot;m from horsepower or kW and speed in rpm, with the 5252 constant explained. Free, no signup.
--intro
<b>Torque in pound-feet is 5,252 times horsepower divided by rpm.</b> In SI units, torque in newton-metres is 9,549 times kilowatts divided by rpm. For a given power, a slower shaft carries more torque, which is why a six-pole motor is physically larger than a two-pole motor of the same rating.
The 5,252 is not a property of motors. It comes from the definition of horsepower, 33,000 foot-pounds per minute, divided by 2&pi; to turn revolutions into radians.
--viz
motor
--formula
T (lb-ft) = 5,252 &times; hp / rpm
T (N&middot;m) = 9,549 &times; kW / rpm
--vars
T :: Torque at the shaft
hp, kW :: Shaft output power
rpm :: Shaft speed in revolutions per minute
--example
A 50 hp motor running at its full-load speed of 1,775 rpm:
T = 5,252 &times; 50 / 1,775 = 147.9 lb-ft, which is 200.6 N&middot;m
A 50 hp motor with six poles runs near 1,180 rpm and delivers 222.5 lb-ft: the same power, half again the torque.
--standard
The relation is definitional. The torque figures that matter for applying a motor, locked-rotor, pull-up and breakdown torque as percentages of full-load torque, are set for each design letter by NEMA MG 1, and by IEC 60034-12 for IEC motors.
--limits
This is full-load running torque at the speed you enter. It says nothing about the torque available during starting, which follows its own curve from locked-rotor torque at standstill, through a dip, up to breakdown torque near full speed. On a variable speed drive, a motor can generally deliver rated torque up to base speed and less above it, where it runs at constant power.
--mistakes
Using synchronous speed in place of full-load speed. Confusing full-load torque with starting torque when checking whether a load will break away. Mixing horsepower with the SI constant or kilowatts with 5,252. Forgetting that a gearbox multiplies torque by its ratio and divides speed by the same figure.

===motor-speed|motor-speed|Motor Speed &amp; Slip Calculator|Motor Speed Calculator (Synchronous RPM &amp; Slip)|Calculate an AC motor's synchronous speed from frequency and poles, and its slip from the nameplate full-load rpm. Free, no signup.
--intro
<b>Synchronous speed in rpm is 120 times the supply frequency divided by the number of poles.</b> At 60 Hz that gives 3,600 rpm for two poles, 1,800 for four, 1,200 for six and 900 for eight. At 50 Hz the same motors run at 3,000, 1,500, 1,000 and 750.
An induction motor never quite reaches that speed. The rotor has to turn slower than the rotating field for current to be induced in it, and the shortfall is called slip. A four-pole motor with a nameplate speed of 1,750 rpm is slipping by 50 rpm, or 2.8 percent, at full load.
--viz
motor
--formula
N&#8347; = 120 &times; f / P
Slip (%) = (N&#8347; &minus; N) / N&#8347; &times; 100
--vars
N&#8347; :: Synchronous speed in rpm
f :: Supply frequency in hertz
P :: Number of poles, always an even number
N :: Actual rotor speed in rpm, from the nameplate at full load
--example
A four-pole motor on a 60 Hz supply:
N&#8347; = 120 &times; 60 / 4 = 1,800 rpm
With a nameplate full-load speed of 1,750 rpm:
Slip = (1,800 &minus; 1,750) / 1,800 &times; 100 = 2.78%
On a variable speed drive set to 48 Hz, the same motor's synchronous speed is 1,440 rpm.
--standard
The relation follows from how a rotating magnetic field is produced and is not set by a standard. Nameplate speed, frequency and the tolerances on slip are covered by NEMA MG 1 and IEC 60034-1.
--limits
Slip is not a fixed property of a motor. It is close to zero at no load and grows roughly in proportion to torque up to full load, so the nameplate figure applies only at rated load, voltage and frequency. Synchronous motors and permanent-magnet motors run at synchronous speed with no slip at all. The pole count is fixed by the winding and cannot be read off the frame.
--mistakes
Expecting an induction motor to run at exactly 1,800 or 3,600 rpm. Entering the number of pole pairs where the formula wants poles. Using the full-load slip to estimate speed at light load. Assuming a motor moved from 60 Hz to 50 Hz simply runs slower, with no effect on its voltage requirement or cooling.

===voltage-unbalance|voltage-unbalance|Voltage Unbalance Calculator|Voltage Unbalance Calculator (NEMA Method)|Calculate three-phase voltage unbalance from three line-to-line readings by the NEMA method, with the effect on motor heating. Free, no signup.
--intro
<b>Voltage unbalance, by the NEMA definition, is the largest difference between any one line-to-line voltage and the average of the three, divided by that average.</b> Readings of 460, 467 and 450 V average 459 V; the furthest from the average is 450 V, 9 V away, so the unbalance is 1.96 percent.
A small unbalance in voltage produces a much larger unbalance in motor current, commonly six to ten times as large in percentage terms, and the extra current turns into heat in the windings. This is why a figure that looks trivial is taken seriously.
--viz
motor
--formula
Unbalance (%) = maximum deviation from average / average &times; 100
--vars
Average :: The mean of the three line-to-line voltages
Maximum deviation :: The largest difference between any one reading and that average
--example
Line-to-line readings of 460 V, 467 V and 450 V:
Average = (460 + 467 + 450) / 3 = 459 V
Deviations are 1 V, 8 V and 9 V, so the maximum is 9 V
Unbalance = 9 / 459 &times; 100 = 1.96%
A common rule of thumb puts the added winding temperature rise at about twice the square of the percentage unbalance: 2 &times; 1.96&sup2; is roughly 8 percent more temperature rise at full load.
--standard
NEMA MG 1 defines this method and states that motors are designed to operate at rated load with up to 1 percent voltage unbalance. Above that it gives a derating curve, and it does not recommend operation above 5 percent. ANSI C84.1 recommends that utility systems be designed to limit unbalance to 3 percent at no load. IEC standards define unbalance differently, from the ratio of negative to positive sequence voltage, and the two methods give similar but not identical figures.
--limits
The NEMA method uses magnitudes only and ignores phase angle, so it is an approximation to the true sequence-component unbalance. It is taken from line-to-line voltages; using line-to-neutral readings gives a different and usually larger number. Readings should be taken at the motor terminals with the motor running, because unbalance caused by a poor connection only appears under load. The temperature-rise rule is an estimate, and the derating a specific motor needs comes from the NEMA curve or its manufacturer.
--mistakes
Dividing the spread between highest and lowest reading by the average, which overstates the result. Using line-to-neutral voltages. Measuring at the switchboard and not at the motor. Chasing an unbalance in the supply when the cause is a single-phase load, a blown capacitor fuse or a high-resistance connection downstream.
