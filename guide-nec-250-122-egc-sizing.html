NEC 250.122: Sizing the Equipment Grounding Conductor

The equipment grounding conductor is sized from the overcurrent device, not from the load, because what it has to survive is a fault rather than a duty cycle. Two provisions around that table account for most of the errors.

By Voltfield Editorial Team·Published November 3, 2026·How we source & verify this

Key takeaways

  • The EGC is sized from the rating of the overcurrent device ahead of the circuit, not from the load and not from the phase conductor.
  • If the ungrounded conductors are upsized for any reason, including voltage drop, the EGC must be increased proportionally by circular mil area.
  • The EGC is never required to be larger than the ungrounded circuit conductors it runs with.
  • Representative copper sizes: 15 A takes 14 AWG, 100 A takes 8 AWG, 200 A takes 6 AWG, 400 A takes 3 AWG.

Why the device, and not the load

Every other conductor in the circuit is sized from what it carries continuously. The equipment grounding conductor carries nothing until something fails, and then it carries the entire available fault current for as long as the protective device takes to open. That is the duty it is sized for.

So NEC 250.122(A) sizes it from the rating or setting of the overcurrent device ahead of the circuit, through Table 250.122. A 100 A breaker gets an 8 AWG copper EGC whether the load behind it is 95 amps or 12. Sizing it from the load, which looks reasonable, produces a conductor that may not survive long enough for the breaker to clear.

Representative copper values from that table: 15 A takes 14 AWG, 20 A takes 12 AWG, 60 A takes 10 AWG, 100 A takes 8 AWG, 200 A takes 6 AWG, 400 A takes 3 AWG, 600 A takes 1 AWG. Aluminum runs larger at each step, and the table itself is the authority rather than this summary.

The upsizing rule in 250.122(B)

This is the provision most often missed, and it is missed because it is triggered by a decision made for an entirely unrelated reason.

250.122(B) says that where ungrounded conductors are increased in size from the minimum that would have sufficient ampacity, the equipment grounding conductor must be increased in size proportionally, by circular mil area.

The usual trigger is voltage drop. A 100 A feeder needs 3 AWG copper on ampacity, the run is long, so the designer specifies 1/0 to hold the drop inside 3 percent. The breaker is still 100 A, so the table still says 8 AWG — but the phase conductors went from 52,620 to 105,600 circular mils, a ratio of about 2.0, and the EGC has to grow by the same ratio. 8 AWG is 16,510 circular mils, so the EGC needs at least 33,020, which is 4 AWG.

Nothing about the installation signals this. The breaker did not change, the load did not change, and the table lookup gives the same answer it always did. Only the reason for the larger phase conductor triggers it.

The ceiling, and parallel runs

Two limits keep the result sensible. First, 250.122(A) provides that the EGC need not be larger than the ungrounded circuit conductors it accompanies — on a small circuit with a large device the table could otherwise ask for more copper in the ground than in the phases.

Second, parallel runs. Where circuit conductors are run in parallel in multiple raceways, 250.122(F) requires an equipment grounding conductor in each raceway, and each one is sized from the overcurrent device protecting the circuit — not from the device rating divided by the number of raceways. The reasoning is straightforward once stated: a fault in one raceway is cleared by the one device, so the EGC in that raceway sees the whole fault.

Where a cable tray or a metal raceway is itself the equipment grounding conductor, 250.118 lists what qualifies, and the sizing question changes shape entirely.

What the EGC is not for

Two things commonly loaded onto this conductor do not belong on it. It is not a neutral: it is bonded to the grounded conductor at exactly one point, and using it to return load current puts normal current on every enclosure in the path. Ground vs. neutral covers that separately.

It is also not the grounding electrode conductor. The GEC connects the system to earth and is sized from Table 250.66 against the service conductors; the EGC connects equipment back to the source and is sized from Table 250.122 against the overcurrent device. They are different conductors, sized from different tables, doing different jobs, and the similar names cause real errors in submittals.

Common mistakes

  • Sizing from the load. The table is indexed on the overcurrent device, because the fault is the duty.
  • Missing 250.122(B). Upsizing phase conductors for voltage drop obliges a proportional increase in the EGC, and nothing in the lookup reminds you.
  • Dividing the device rating across parallel raceways. Each raceway's EGC is sized from the full device rating.
  • Confusing Table 250.122 with Table 250.66. One sizes the equipment grounding conductor, the other the grounding electrode conductor.
  • Using the EGC as a neutral. A different fault with the same symptom: current on metal that should never carry any.

Frequently asked questions

How is the equipment grounding conductor sized?

From the rating or setting of the overcurrent device ahead of the circuit, using NEC Table 250.122 — not from the load and not from the phase conductor size. The EGC carries fault current only, for as long as the device takes to open, so the device rating is what determines the duty. Representative copper sizes are 14 AWG at 15 A, 8 AWG at 100 A and 6 AWG at 200 A.

Do I have to upsize the ground wire if I upsize the conductors?

Yes. NEC 250.122(B) requires the equipment grounding conductor to be increased proportionally by circular mil area whenever the ungrounded conductors are increased above the minimum size that would have had sufficient ampacity. Upsizing a feeder for voltage drop is the usual trigger, and the overcurrent device rating does not change, so the table lookup alone will not reveal it.

Can the EGC be smaller than the phase conductors?

Usually yes, and that is normal: a 100 A circuit on 3 AWG copper takes an 8 AWG copper EGC. The code also caps it in the other direction, since NEC 250.122(A) provides that the equipment grounding conductor need not be larger than the ungrounded circuit conductors it runs with.

Is the EGC the same as the grounding electrode conductor?

No. The equipment grounding conductor bonds equipment back to the source so fault current can clear a device, and is sized from Table 250.122 against the overcurrent device. The grounding electrode conductor connects the system to earth and is sized from Table 250.66 against the service conductors. Different conductors, different tables, different purposes.
