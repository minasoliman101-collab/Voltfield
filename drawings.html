Electrical Drawings Library: Typical Single-Line Diagrams

Typical single-line diagrams you can read, download and adapt, the symbols they use, and a plain guide to the kinds of drawing an equipment order produces.

A single-line diagram shows an entire power system on one sheet by drawing one line for all three phases. It is the drawing that engineers size equipment from, that purchasers read to see what is being bought, and that everyone points at in a meeting. Each drawing here is a typical arrangement with every element explained, free to download as a vector file.

Typical single-line diagrams

SUB

Substation single-line diagram

One transmission line, one power transformer, a medium-voltage bus and three feeders, with station service.

 PV

Solar plant single-line diagram

PV arrays, inverters and pad-mounted transformers on a collector bus, stepped up once more to the grid.

 BESS

Battery storage single-line diagram

Battery blocks behind bidirectional converters on a medium-voltage bus, with the auxiliary supply that keeps them cool.

 DC

Data center single-line diagram

Utility and generator through a transfer switch, then UPS, PDU and racks, with the cooling plant on the same board.

To build and check one of your own, use the one-line sandbox.

Symbols used here

Symbol conventions vary between companies and between North American and IEC practice. A real drawing set includes its own legend sheet, and that legend governs. Relay and device numbers are covered in ANSI device numbers.

Kinds of drawing, and who needs each

DrawingWhat it showsWho uses it
Single-line diagram (one-line)The whole power system with one line standing for all three phases: sources, transformers, breakers, buses and loads, with ratings.Everyone. It is the first drawing made and the one every study starts from.
Three-line diagramThe same circuit with every phase and the neutral drawn, showing instrument transformer connections and phasing.Protection engineers, and the people wiring CTs, VTs and meters.
Schematic (elementary) diagramHow a control circuit works, laid out by function: what closes a breaker, what trips it, what interlocks it.Controls engineers and commissioning technicians.
Wiring (connection) diagramEvery wire and terminal as physically installed, device by device.Panel builders and electricians.
General arrangement and outlinePhysical size, weight, anchoring, cable entry and clearances of one piece of equipment.Civil and structural design, logistics and the installing contractor.
Layout and plan drawingsWhere equipment sits in a room or yard, with working space and egress.Design coordination and the authority having jurisdiction.
Cable and conduit scheduleEvery cable: from, to, size, type, length and route.Estimators, purchasers and installers.
Grounding planThe grounding electrode system, grid conductors and bonding connections.Electrical and civil crews, and the testing firm.
P&IDPiping and instrumentation diagram: process piping, valves and instruments for cooling water, fuel and other fluid systems.Mechanical and controls engineers.
Nameplate and bill of materials drawingsWhat the nameplate will state and every component in the assembly.Whoever checks the delivery against the order.

Drawings in an equipment order

When equipment is bought, drawings move in a fixed sequence, and each step is a point where schedule is won or lost.

  • Approval drawings. The vendor submits outline, nameplate, single-line and schematic drawings after the order. Nothing should be built until the buyer releases them. Put the due date and the buyer’s turnaround in the purchase order.
  • Review status. Drawings come back approved, approved as noted, or revise and resubmit. Each resubmittal costs weeks. Track them in the expediting log.
  • Certified for construction. The released set that the factory builds to and the site designs foundations and cable entries from.
  • As-built. The final set, corrected for every change made in the factory and at the factory acceptance test. It is a deliverable, and worth tying to the last payment.
