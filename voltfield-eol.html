Finding a replacement for a discontinued electrical part

Equipment outlives the catalogue it was bought from. A breaker installed in 1998 is still protecting a circuit today, and when it finally fails the part number on its label has usually been obsolete for a decade. This tool maps discontinued platforms to what a manufacturer currently offers in their place, so the search starts from a real successor rather than a dead part number.

The important thing to be clear about: a replacement class is not the same part. It is what the manufacturer positions as the current equivalent, which usually means different dimensions, different accessories and sometimes a different mounting arrangement. It tells you where to look. It does not tell you the part will drop in.

Why manufacturers discontinue platforms

Rarely because the product stopped working. The common drivers are a standards revision that the original design cannot meet without recertification, a component in the supply chain going out of production, or a platform consolidation after an acquisition. The last of these is why so many obsolete lines trace back to a handful of parent companies today.

What actually happens when a platform goes end-of-life

It happens in stages, and the stage matters more than the label. Active production ends first, but factory stock and channel inventory can carry availability for a year or more. Service parts and technical support typically continue beyond that, often for a decade. Only after that does the platform become genuinely unsupported — which is when replacement stops being optional and becomes a project.

Aftermarket and reconditioned parts are a legitimate option, with conditions. For a like-for-like replacement in an existing installation, a reconditioned breaker from a reputable remanufacturer is often the lowest-risk path — it fits the existing enclosure and bus stabs, which a modern equivalent will not. Ask for the test report, the reconditioning standard applied, and what warranty is offered. For anything being newly installed or inspected, check first whether the AHJ will accept it.

Retrofit kits versus full replacement

Many manufacturers sell retrofill or retrofit kits that put current-generation internals into an existing enclosure. These are meaningfully cheaper than replacing a lineup and avoid an extended outage, but they are engineered products in their own right — they need the original enclosure to be in known condition, and they carry their own listing. Where a lineup is otherwise sound, this is usually the option worth pricing first.

How to use the results

  • Confirm the frame and interrupting rating independently. A successor platform can share a name and not share an interrupting rating. That number has to be checked against the available fault current at the installed location, not carried across on the assumption of equivalence.
  • Check the accessories separately. Shunt trips, auxiliary contacts, undervoltage releases and trip units are the parts most likely to be incompatible across a platform change, and they are also the parts most often forgotten until commissioning.
  • Ask what the physical envelope is. A modern equivalent is frequently smaller electrically and different mechanically. In an existing enclosure, mechanical fit governs.
  • Treat lead time as part of the decision. A current-production successor with a forty-week lead time may be a worse answer than a reconditioned original available next week, depending on whether the equipment is running now.

Common questions

Is a discontinued breaker unsafe to keep in service?

Not inherently. Discontinuation is a commercial decision about production, not a safety finding. An installed breaker that tests correctly and is within its maintenance interval is doing its job. What changes is your exposure: when it does fail, the replacement is harder to obtain, and that is an availability risk rather than a safety one.

How long are service parts available after a platform is discontinued?

It varies by manufacturer and product line, and the only reliable answer comes from the manufacturer for that specific platform. Ten years of service parts support after end of production is a common commitment for major electrical equipment, but it is a commitment rather than a rule, and it can be shortened when a supply chain for a sub-component disappears.

Can I mix a new breaker into an old panel or switchgear lineup?

Only where the manufacturer lists that combination. Panelboards and switchgear are listed as assemblies, and the listing covers specific devices in specific positions. Installing an unlisted combination can void the assembly listing regardless of how well it fits mechanically, which is a problem at inspection and a larger one after an incident.

Why does this tool show a replacement class rather than an exact part number?

Because an exact cross-reference would frequently be wrong. Choosing the specific catalogue number requires the frame size, interrupting rating, trip unit, voltage and accessories for your application, and those are not derivable from the obsolete part number alone. The class narrows the search to the right family; the manufacturer completes it against your actual conditions.
