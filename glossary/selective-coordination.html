Glossary

Selective coordination

Arranging protective devices so that only the one nearest a fault opens, leaving everything upstream energized.

Definition

Arranging protective devices so that only the one nearest a fault opens, leaving everything upstream energized. It is demonstrated by comparing time-current curves across the whole path, which is why it is a system property rather than a device setting. See the short-circuit and coordination guide.

In practice

When a fault occurs, every protective device between it and the source sees the fault current. In a coordinated system the device immediately upstream of the fault opens first and the others stay closed, so only the faulted circuit is lost. Without coordination a fault on one branch can open a feeder or a main and take down everything it serves.

It is achieved by making each upstream device slower than the one below it across the whole range of fault currents, which is checked by overlaying their time-current curves. The difficulty is at high fault currents, where breakers trip instantaneously and cannot wait for each other. That is solved with fuses, with breakers that have a short-time delay in place of an instantaneous trip, or with zone-selective interlocking, where breakers signal one another.

Worth knowing

  • Delaying upstream devices to gain coordination increases arc-flash energy, so the two aims have to be balanced.
  • Codes require it for certain systems, including emergency systems, legally required standby systems and elevators.
