EV Load Management: NEC 625.42 and ALMS

EV charging enters a load calculation at full value with no demand factor, which is what makes the arithmetic so tight. An automatic load management system changes what the service has to be sized for, and the word doing the work is enforceable.

By Voltfield Editorial Team·Published November 3, 2026·How we source & verify this

Key takeaways

  • EV supply equipment enters a service calculation at the larger of 7,200 VA or its nameplate, at 100 percent, with no demand factor.
  • Article 625 treats EV charging as a continuous load, which is why no demand factor is available.
  • With an automatic load management system, the calculated load is the maximum the system permits rather than the sum of the nameplates.
  • The system has to actually constrain the load, not merely schedule or recommend. That is the difference between a compliant design and a story.

Why the arithmetic is tight

Most appliance loads get some relief in a service calculation. Several ranges, several dryers, a row of fixed appliances — Article 220 applies demand factors on the reasonable assumption that they will not all run flat out at once.

EV charging gets none of that, and the reason is in Article 625: EV charging is treated as a continuous load. A car plugged in overnight draws its full current for hours, which is exactly what a demand factor assumes will not happen. So the equipment enters the calculation at the larger of 7,200 VA or its nameplate rating, at 100 percent.

Note that 7,200 VA is a floor rather than a value. A 40 A unit at 240 V is 9,600 VA and enters at 9,600. And the figure is per unit, so two chargers present as two full loads with nothing deducted for the near-certainty that only one car is usually charging.

Put a second charger on a dwelling that already has a heat pump and a range and the service calculation frequently fails. That is not a conservative result produced by a cautious engineer; it is what the rules require when applied straightforwardly.

What load management changes

NEC 625.42 provides the route out, and it is worth being precise about what it does.

Where an automatic load management system is installed, the maximum equipment load on a service or feeder is permitted to be the maximum load the management system allows, rather than the sum of the connected equipment ratings. The calculation is performed against the enforced limit.

So four 48 A chargers behind a system configured and listed to hold the group to 48 A total present to the service as 48 A, not 192 A. The hardware is unchanged; what changed is that there is now something guaranteeing the aggregate cannot exceed the figure the service was sized for.

This is the same logic as power control systems for interconnection under NEC 705.13, and recognising that the two are the same idea makes both easier to reason about: in each case a listed control system substitutes an enforced limit for a nameplate sum.

What makes a system enforceable

The provision rests entirely on the word automatic, and this is where designs fail review.

A system qualifies by actually constraining the load. It has to monitor, and it has to be able to reduce or interrupt charging current to hold the total at or below the configured limit, without relying on anyone doing anything. The limit has to be a setting the system enforces, not a target it aims at.

Things that are not load management, despite being sold alongside it: a scheduling feature that charges overnight, because nothing stops two cars charging overnight simultaneously. An app that notifies the owner. A written operating procedure. A timer. Each of these reduces the likelihood of coincident load and none of them reduces the maximum, and the calculation is about the maximum.

The practical consequences are that the system has to be listed for the purpose, the configured limit has to be documented and field marked so an inspector can see what the service was sized to, and the limit has to be set before the installation is signed off rather than left at a default.

When it beats a service upgrade

The comparison is usually straightforward once the numbers are on the table.

A service upgrade means a new service, frequently a new meter base and panel, utility coordination, and on a constrained distribution network a wait that can run to many months. Load management means equipment and configuration, and no utility involvement at all. Where the existing service has some headroom and the shortfall is created by the EV load alone, load management is almost always faster and cheaper.

It is also the answer that scales for multi-unit buildings, where the alternative is sizing a service for every parking space at full value. A garage with thirty spaces cannot realistically be served by thirty full charger loads, and load management is what makes the project exist at all rather than merely cheaper.

Where it does not help: if the service is already at its limit from the non-EV load, managing the EV load down to nothing still leaves the service inadequate. Load management solves a coincidence problem, not a capacity problem, and establishing which one you have is what NEC 220.87 is for.

Common mistakes

Applying a demand factor to EV charging, which Article 625 does not permit because the load is continuous. Reading 7,200 VA as the value rather than the floor, and understating a 48 A unit. Treating scheduling, timers or an app notification as load management, when none of them limits the maximum. Leaving the system at a default limit instead of configuring and documenting the figure the service was sized to. Omitting the field marking, so nobody can later tell what the service capacity assumed. Expecting load management to rescue a service that is already inadequate from its existing load. Sizing the branch circuit from the managed limit rather than the individual equipment rating, which is a separate calculation.

Frequently asked questions

Does an EV charger get a demand factor in a load calculation?

No. Article 625 treats EV charging as a continuous load, so the equipment enters a service or feeder calculation at the larger of 7,200 VA or its nameplate rating, at 100 percent. The 7,200 VA figure is a floor, not a value: a 40 A unit at 240 V is 9,600 VA and enters at 9,600. Two units present as two full loads.

What does NEC 625.42 allow?

Where an automatic load management system is installed, the maximum equipment load on a service or feeder is permitted to be the maximum load the management system allows, rather than the sum of the connected equipment nameplate ratings. Four 48 A chargers held by a listed system to 48 A total present to the service as 48 A.

Does a scheduling feature count as load management?

No. Scheduling, timers, app notifications and written procedures all reduce the likelihood of coincident load without reducing the maximum, and the calculation is about the maximum. A qualifying system has to monitor and be able to reduce or interrupt charging current to hold the total at or below its configured limit, automatically, without anyone intervening.

Is load management better than upgrading the service?

Usually, where the existing service has headroom and the shortfall comes from the EV load alone: it avoids utility coordination and the wait that goes with it. It is also what makes multi-unit garages feasible, since sizing a service for every space at full value rarely is. It does not help if the service is already inadequate from its existing load, which is a capacity problem rather than a coincidence problem.

Based on

What it is not
