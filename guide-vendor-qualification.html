How to Qualify an Equipment Vendor: Prequalification and Scorecards

A bid is a promise. Vendor qualification is the work of finding out whether the company making it can keep it: build the equipment, test it, deliver it on the date and still be there when the warranty is called.

By Voltfield Editorial Team·Published November 15, 2026·How we source & verify this

Key takeaways

  • Qualify vendors before the request for quotation goes out. A bid from a vendor you would not award to wastes everyone’s time and distorts the comparison.
  • Check six things: technical capability, quality system, track record, capacity, financial standing and compliance.
  • A reference is only useful if it is for the same kind of equipment at a similar rating, and if you speak to the person who received it.
  • After award, measure the vendor on a few facts: dates met, drawing turnaround, test results, and how problems were handled.

Why qualify before bidding

Vendor qualification is the check, made before a company is invited to bid, that it is able to supply what you need to the standard you need. The companies that pass go on an approved vendor list, sometimes called an approved manufacturers list, and bids are invited from that list.

Doing it first matters for a practical reason. Once prices are on the table, an unqualified low bid is very hard to set aside. It pulls the budget expectation down and puts pressure on the qualified bidders, and if it is accepted the project inherits the risk. Long-lead equipment makes this worse: by the time a vendor’s shortcomings show, the alternative is another full manufacturing cycle. See transformer lead times for how long that can be.

Six things to check

1. Technical capability

Has the vendor built this type of equipment at this rating and voltage class, in its own factory? Ask for a supply record of similar units with ratings and dates. Ask where the equipment will be built, since a company with several plants may qualify at one and not another. Ask what the factory can test in-house: a manufacturer that cannot perform the tests your specification requires will be sending the unit elsewhere or asking for a waiver. See FAT vs. SAT.

2. Quality system

Certification to ISO 9001 shows that a quality management system exists and is audited. It is a starting point. What tells you more is how the vendor handles nonconformances, how it controls its own suppliers of critical parts such as core steel, bushings, breakers and relays, and whether the product carries the listings and certifications your project requires. For major equipment a factory audit, in person, is worth the trip.

3. Track record

Ask for references for comparable equipment and call them. Ask the person who received the equipment whether it arrived on the acknowledged date, how the drawings and the factory test went, what failed in service, and how the vendor responded. Ask whether they would order again.

4. Capacity

A capable factory with a full order book will still deliver late. Ask for the current lead time and what it is measured from, how much of the plant’s capacity is already committed, and which components the vendor is itself waiting on.

5. Financial standing

Equipment orders involve advance payments and a warranty that runs for years. Both depend on the vendor staying in business. Review financial statements or a credit report in proportion to the size of the order, and consider security for advance payments, such as a bank guarantee or a bond, where the exposure is large.

6. Compliance and support

Confirm what the project requires: country of origin and domestic content, covered in the Buy America and FEOC guides, safety record for any site work, insurance, and export or sanctions restrictions. Then ask who answers the phone after delivery: where the service engineers are based, where spares are held and how long the vendor has supported earlier product generations.

The prequalification questionnaire

The usual tool is a questionnaire sent to each prospective vendor, with the same questions for all and a request for evidence, not statements. Keep it short enough that a good vendor will answer it properly. A useful one asks for:

  • Legal entity, ownership, and the manufacturing location proposed
  • Supply record for comparable equipment over the last five years
  • Test facilities and their limits
  • Quality certificates and product listings, with certificate numbers and expiry dates
  • Sources of critical components
  • Current lead time and capacity
  • Three references with contact details
  • Financial statements or a credit reference
  • Warranty terms and service locations

Score the answers against criteria you set beforehand, with a pass mark, and record the result. Some criteria are pass or fail by nature: a vendor that cannot meet a legal content requirement does not qualify however well it scores elsewhere.

After award: the vendor scorecard

Qualification is a forecast. Performance on an order is the evidence. A scorecard records that evidence in a form that can be compared across vendors and fed back into the approved list. It works best with a handful of measures that come from dates and documents already on file:

MeasureHow to take it
On-time deliveryActual delivery date against the date acknowledged on the order
Drawing turnaroundDays from order to first submittal, and number of resubmittals
Quality at the factoryFAT passed first time or not; punch list items at release
Quality on arrivalExceptions on the proof of delivery; defects found at site testing
DocumentationTest reports and manuals complete and on time
ResponsivenessTime to answer technical queries and to resolve warranty claims
Commercial conductChange orders raised, and whether they were justified

Share the scorecard with the vendor. Most will improve what they know is being measured, and a vendor that disputes a date is giving you a chance to correct your own records. Review the approved list on a schedule, since factories change hands, key people leave and a vendor that was excellent three years ago may not be now.

Common mistakes

  • Qualifying the brand and not the factory.
  • Accepting references without calling them.
  • Treating a quality certificate as proof of quality.
  • Adding an unqualified vendor to the bid list “to keep the others honest”.
  • Never removing anyone from the approved list.
  • Scoring vendors on impressions. Use dates and documents.

Frequently asked questions

What is vendor prequalification?

The process of checking, before inviting bids, that a supplier has the technical capability, quality system, track record, capacity, financial standing and compliance to supply what is needed. Suppliers that pass go on an approved vendor list.

What is an approved vendor list?

A list of suppliers that an organization has qualified to supply a given type of equipment. Bids are invited from the list, and the list is reviewed as vendors’ performance and circumstances change. It is also called an approved manufacturers list.

What should a vendor scorecard measure?

A small number of factual measures: on-time delivery against the acknowledged date, drawing turnaround, factory test results, condition on arrival, completeness of documentation, responsiveness, and the number and merit of change orders.

Is ISO 9001 certification enough to qualify a manufacturer?

No. It shows that a quality management system exists and is audited, which is a reasonable minimum. It does not show that the manufacturer has built your type of equipment, can test it, has capacity or is financially sound.

Next steps

Qualified vendors receive the request for quotation, their bids go into the tabulation, and the order records what was agreed. Performance against that order is what the scorecard measures.
