Motor Full-Load Current Chart

Full-load current by horsepower for three-phase and single-phase motors, as used to size motor circuit conductors and protection.

For sizing a motor circuit, the full-load current comes from the code table for the motor's horsepower and voltage, not from its nameplate. A 50 hp three-phase motor is taken as 65 A at 460 V and 130 A at 230 V. The table figure is used for the conductors, the short-circuit protection and the disconnect; the nameplate current is used only for the overload protection.

How to read it

Find the motor's horsepower and read across to its voltage. The 230 V column also serves motors rated 220 to 240 V, the 460 V column those rated 440 to 480 V, and the 575 V column those rated 550 to 600 V.

Branch-circuit conductors for a single motor are sized at 125 percent of this current. For a 50 hp, 460 V motor that is 65 × 1.25 = 81.3 A, and the conductor is then chosen from the ampacity table.

The same current is the base for the short-circuit and ground-fault protective device, which is sized as a percentage of it that depends on the type of device, and is deliberately well above the running current so that the motor can start.

Overload protection is the one thing sized from the nameplate. It protects the motor itself, so it follows the current that particular motor draws.

Where the values come from

The three-phase currents correspond to NEC Table 430.250 and the single-phase currents to Table 430.248. They are reproduced here for reference and are not computed by Voltfield. The figures are the same pattern throughout: the 230 V current is twice the 460 V current, and the 208 V current is ten percent higher than the 230 V one. Check any value you intend to rely on against the edition of the code your jurisdiction has adopted.

These are code values, not Voltfield's. NEC and National Electrical Code are trademarks of the National Fire Protection Association, which publishes the code. Voltfield is not affiliated with it. The published code, in the edition adopted where you are, is the authority.

What this chart does not tell you

These are code values for sizing, and they are deliberately conservative. An actual motor, particularly a high-efficiency one, usually draws less. The tables do not cover low-speed motors of 1,200 rpm or less, high-torque motors or multispeed motors, which can draw more and are sized from the nameplate. They do not give locked-rotor current, and they do not size the overload device.

This is a reference, not a design. It is here to give you the right order of magnitude and a figure to check against. It does not replace the adopted code or a calculation by a qualified engineer.

Frequently asked questions

How many amps does a 10 hp motor draw?

For circuit sizing, a 10 hp three-phase motor is taken as 14 A at 460 V, 28 A at 230 V and 30.8 A at 208 V. A single-phase 10 hp motor at 230 V is taken as 50 A.

Why not just use the nameplate current?

A motor can be replaced with another of the same horsepower that draws more. Sizing the conductors and short-circuit protection from the table means the circuit remains adequate for any standard motor of that rating. The nameplate current is used for overload protection, which is matched to the motor actually installed.

What size wire for a 50 hp motor at 460 V?

The table current is 65 A. At 125 percent the conductor needs an ampacity of 81.3 A, which 4 AWG copper provides in the 75°C column at 85 A. Ambient temperature, bundling and voltage drop can require a larger size.

Is full-load current the same as starting current?

No. A motor started across the line draws roughly six times its full-load current for a few seconds, and more for some designs. That is why the short-circuit protection is set well above full-load current while the overload protection is set close to it.
