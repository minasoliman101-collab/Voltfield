Voltage Drop Chart

How far each copper wire size can run before it loses 3 percent of the voltage, at common currents and voltages.

For a 3 percent drop, the longest one-way run is 0.03 times the voltage times the wire area, divided by the current and a constant. At 120 V, 12 AWG copper carrying 20 A reaches the 3 percent mark at 45 ft. The same wire on a 240 V circuit goes twice as far, because the allowed drop in volts is twice as large.

Metric tables are shown. The worked examples and formulas further down the page are in US units.

How to read it

Pick the table for your voltage, find the current across the top, and read down to the first wire size whose length is at least the length of your run. Lengths are one way, from source to load.

A dash means the current is more than that conductor may carry, so the wire is ruled out before voltage drop is even considered. On short runs ampacity decides the wire size; on long runs voltage drop does.

For a 5 percent limit, multiply any length by 1.67. For aluminum, multiply by 0.61. For a different voltage on the same kind of circuit, scale the length in proportion: a 208 V single-phase run is 208/240 of the 240 V figure.

How the values are calculated

L = (0.03 × V × cmil) / (2 × 12.9 × I)  [single-phase]
L = (0.03 × V × cmil) / (1.732 × 12.9 × I)  [three-phase]

Every figure in the chart comes from the formulas above. Nothing is copied from a published table, so any cell can be checked by hand.

What this chart does not tell you

The K-factor method uses resistance only. It ignores reactance, which matters on large conductors and long runs, so the lengths for the biggest sizes are optimistic. It assumes copper at about 75°C and a load at the far end. The 3 percent figure is a recommendation in the NEC informational notes for a branch circuit or a feeder, not a requirement, though some equipment and some local codes do require it.

This is a reference, not a design. It is here to give you the right order of magnitude and a figure to check against. It does not replace the adopted code or a calculation by a qualified engineer.

Frequently asked questions

How far can I run 12 gauge wire on a 20 amp circuit?

At 120 V, 12 AWG copper carrying a full 20 A reaches a 3 percent voltage drop at about 45 ft one way. At 16 A, the continuous limit for that circuit, it reaches about 57 ft. Beyond that, go up to 10 AWG.

Is 3 percent voltage drop a code requirement?

In the NEC it is a recommendation, given in informational notes: 3 percent for a branch circuit or a feeder and 5 percent for the two combined. It becomes a requirement where a local code adopts it, and for some specific equipment such as fire pumps.

Why does a higher voltage allow a longer run?

The allowed drop is a percentage of the voltage. Three percent of 240 V is 7.2 V, twice the 3.6 V allowed at 120 V, and for the same power the current is lower as well. That is the main reason long feeders are run at higher voltage.
