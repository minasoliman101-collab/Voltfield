Wire Ampacity Chart

Allowable ampacity for copper and aluminum conductors at the three insulation temperature ratings, and the factors that reduce it.

Ampacity is the current a conductor can carry continuously without exceeding its temperature rating, and it depends on the insulation rating as much as on the wire size. A 6 AWG copper conductor is good for 55 A at 60°C, 65 A at 75°C and 75 A at 90°C. Those figures hold for not more than three current-carrying conductors together at an ambient of 30°C (86°F). Anything hotter or more crowded reduces them.

How to read it

Find the wire size and read across to the column for the temperature rating that governs. That is usually not the insulation rating. Equipment terminals are rated 60°C or 75°C, and the conductor must not carry more than its ampacity in the column matching the lowest-rated termination. In general, circuits of 100 A or less use the 60°C column unless the equipment is marked for 75°C, and larger circuits use 75°C.

A 90°C conductor earns its higher figure in a different way. Its 90°C ampacity is the starting point for the correction factors, so it absorbs a hot ambient or a crowded raceway better. Take 4 AWG copper with 90°C insulation at 40°C ambient with six current-carrying conductors: 95 × 0.91 × 0.80 = 69.2 A, which is then compared with the 85 A the 75°C termination column allows. The smaller figure, 69.2 A, is the ampacity.

The smallest sizes have a further limit that the table does not show. Whatever their ampacity, the overcurrent device is generally limited to 15 A for 14 AWG copper, 20 A for 12 AWG and 30 A for 10 AWG.

A continuous load is taken at 125 percent before any of this, so a conductor for an 80 A continuous load needs an ampacity of 100 A.

Where the values come from

The ampacities correspond to NEC Table 310.16, the ambient correction factors to Table 310.15(B)(1), and the adjustment factors to Table 310.15(C)(1). They are reproduced here for reference and are not computed by Voltfield. Editions of the code differ, table numbers have changed between editions, and local amendments apply, so check any value you intend to rely on against the edition your jurisdiction has adopted.

These are code values, not Voltfield's. NEC and National Electrical Code are trademarks of the National Fire Protection Association, which publishes the code. Voltfield is not affiliated with it. The published code, in the edition adopted where you are, is the authority.

What this chart does not tell you

The chart covers conductors in a raceway, cable or directly buried, up to 500 kcmil. It does not cover conductors in free air, which carry more; cables in tray, flexible cords, or conductors rated above 2,000 V, which have their own tables; or the rooftop and other special conditions the code treats separately. It does not tell you the overcurrent device, and it does not check voltage drop, which on a long run sets the size before ampacity does.

This is a reference, not a design. It is here to give you the right order of magnitude and a figure to check against. It does not replace the adopted code or a calculation by a qualified engineer.

Frequently asked questions

What size wire do I need for 100 amps?

For a 100 A circuit on 75°C terminations, 3 AWG copper (100 A) or 1 AWG aluminum (100 A) meets the ampacity before any correction. If the 100 A is a continuous load, the conductor needs 125 A, which is 1 AWG copper or 2/0 aluminum. Hot ambient or bundled conductors can push the size up further.

Why are there three ampacities for the same wire?

Each column is for an insulation temperature rating. Insulation that tolerates more heat lets the same copper carry more current. Which column you may use depends on the insulation and, more often, on the temperature rating of the terminals it lands on.

Can I use the 90°C column?

Only for applying correction and adjustment factors, in most installations. Terminals are rarely rated above 75°C, so the final ampacity cannot exceed the 60°C or 75°C figure for that size. The 90°C value is the starting point for derating, not the answer.

How much does bundling reduce ampacity?

With four to six current-carrying conductors together, ampacity falls to 80 percent. With seven to nine it is 70 percent, and with ten to twenty it is 50 percent. A neutral that carries only unbalanced current is generally not counted.
