kW to Amps Chart

Current for a given real power at the common three-phase and single-phase voltages.

Three-phase amps are kW times 1,000, divided by 1.732 times the voltage times the power factor. At 480 V and a power factor of 0.9, each kW draws about 1.34 A, so 100 kW is 134 A. The three-phase table below is worked at 0.9; the note under it says how to adjust for any other value.

How to read it

Find the load in the first column and read across to the system voltage. The three-phase figures are line current, the current in each of the three conductors.

Power factor matters. The same 100 kW at 480 V draws 120 A at unity power factor, 134 A at 0.9 and 150 A at 0.8. If you do not know the power factor of a motor load, 0.85 is a more cautious assumption than 0.9.

How the values are calculated

I = kW × 1000 / (1.732 × V × PF)  [three-phase]
I = kW × 1000 / (V × PF)  [single-phase]

Every figure in the chart comes from the formulas above. Nothing is copied from a published table, so any cell can be checked by hand.

What this chart does not tell you

This is steady-state current at the stated power factor. It is not the current to size a conductor or a breaker from: a continuous load is taken at 125 percent, motors are sized from the code tables and not from nameplate power, and starting current is several times the running figure.

This is a reference, not a design. It is here to give you the right order of magnitude and a figure to check against. It does not replace the adopted code or a calculation by a qualified engineer.

Frequently asked questions

How many amps is 100 kW at 480 V?

About 134 A per phase at a power factor of 0.9, on a three-phase supply. At unity power factor it is 120 A, and at 0.8 it is 150 A.

How many amps is 1 kW?

It depends on the voltage. On single-phase, 1 kW is 8.3 A at 120 V and 4.2 A at 240 V for a resistive load. On three-phase at 0.9 power factor it is 3.1 A at 208 V and 1.3 A at 480 V.

Why is the three-phase current lower than single-phase for the same power?

Three conductors share the power, and the factor of 1.732 in the formula reflects that. For the same voltage and power, the current in each three-phase conductor is the single-phase current divided by 1.732.
