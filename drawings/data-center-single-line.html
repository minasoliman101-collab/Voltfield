Data Center Single-Line Diagram: A Typical Power Path

One power path of a data center, from the utility and the generator to the rack, and where the cooling plant fits.

A typical arrangement for learning. It carries no ratings on purpose and is not a design. Symbols are explained in the legend.

Reading it from the top

Utility service and transformer
The normal source. The service transformer steps utility medium voltage down to the building’s distribution voltage, 480 V or 415 V in North America: see 415 V vs. 480 V.
Standby generator
The alternate source. It has to carry the IT load and the cooling that keeps it alive. See generator sizing.
Automatic transfer switch
Senses loss of utility, signals the generator to start and moves the load to it. The load sees a short break. Larger sites use breaker pairs in switchgear to do the same job. See ATS vs. STS vs. static bypass.
Main switchboard
Divides power between the critical load, the mechanical plant and the building.
UPS and battery
Carries the IT load through the seconds between utility failure and the generator taking over, and cleans up the supply the rest of the time. See UPS topologies and UPS sizing.
PDU transformer
Steps down to the voltage the rack power supplies use and distributes it to rack-level branch circuits.
IT racks
The critical load. Nearly every watt delivered here becomes heat the cooling plant must remove.
Cooling plant
Usually not on the UPS, so it stops when utility fails and restarts on generator. That gap is a design decision in its own right at high rack densities.

What a real single-line adds

A single-line diagram for construction is a controlled document that an engineer is responsible for. Compared with this one it would also show:

  • A second, independent path. This drawing is a single path (N). Real facilities duplicate some or all of it: see N+1 vs. 2N and Uptime Institute tiers
  • UPS static bypass and maintenance bypass
  • Ratings: transformer kVA and impedance, breaker frame and trip, bus ampacity and short-circuit rating
  • Ground-fault protection on the main, and surge protection
  • Protective relays and metering, including the meters behind a PUE figure
  • Static transfer switches or dual-corded loads at the rack
