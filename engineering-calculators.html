Engineering Calculators for Power & Energy Procurement

A full set of client-side calculators for early-stage sizing and budgetary planning — cable sizing, transformer & BESS sizing, solar strings, fault current, arc-flash incident energy, power factor correction, and unit conversion. Standard formulas, your inputs, nothing sent to a server.

These tools are built for the budgetary and early-design stage of a project — sizing a feeder, roughing in a transformer or BESS order, or checking a string configuration before it goes to a stamped design. They run entirely in your browser: nothing you enter is sent anywhere. None of them replace a licensed engineer's calculation for final design, protective device coordination, or code compliance sign-off.

Voltage Drop / Cable Sizing Calculator

Estimates voltage drop across a feeder using the standard K-factor method, then flags runs that exceed the commonly used 3% branch-circuit / 5% total design guideline (NEC 210.19(A) informational note — a recommendation, not a hard limit).

VD = (2 × K × I × D) / CM  [single-phase]  ·  VD = (1.732 × K × I × D) / CM  [three-phase]

Voltage Drop

K = 12.9 (copper) or 21.2 (aluminum) ohm·cmil/ft — approximate, ignores conductor reactance. Best for runs under ~250 ft.

Cable Ampacity & Conduit Fill Calculators

Looks up base conductor ampacity from NEC Table 310.16 (no more than 3 current-carrying conductors, 30°C ambient — before any correction factors) and checks conduit fill against NEC Chapter 9's percentage caps. This covers a curated subset of common sizes for early-stage estimating, not the full code table.

Fill % = 100 × (n × Aconductor) / Aconduit  ·  cap: 53% (1 conductor) · 31% (2) · 40% (3+)

Ampacity Lookup

Base ampacity only — apply NEC 310.15(B) ambient/altitude correction and 310.15(C) bundling adjustment factors for your actual install conditions, and respect the 60°C/75°C termination rating of the equipment per NEC 110.14(C).

Conduit Fill

EMT conduit and THHN/THWN-2 conductor areas, a handful of common sizes only. Mixed conductor sizes in one raceway, or other conduit/conductor types, need NEC Annex C or a real fill calc.
NEC Annex C publishes the code's own pre-computed conductors-per-conduit tables, which can differ by about ±1 conductor from the simple area-ratio math here due to NFPA's own rounding conventions — use Annex C (or a licensed designer) for anything past a rough early estimate, and always confirm against the NEC/NFPA 70 edition actually adopted by your jurisdiction.

Transformer Sizing Calculator

Converts a real load in kW to required kVA and rounds up to the nearest standard ANSI/IEEE C57.12.00 transformer nameplate rating, plus estimated full-load amps on the secondary.

kVA = kW / PF  ·  FLA = kVA × 1000 / (1.732 × V)  [3φ]  or  kVA × 1000 / V  [1φ]

Transformer Size

Rounds up to the nearest standard kVA nameplate rating. Add your own margin for future load growth — this doesn't assume any.

Transformer Inrush Current Estimator

Energizing a transformer briefly draws a magnetizing inrush current well above full-load amps — enough to matter for upstream breaker/fuse selection and protective coordination. The multiplier below is an industry rule of thumb (commonly cited around IEEE 141/242 and manufacturer application literature), not a precise physical constant — it varies by core design, steel grade, residual flux, and point-on-wave of energization.

FLA = kVA × 1000 / (1.732 × V)  [3φ]  ·  Peak inrush ≈ M × FLA

Inrush Current

Pick the size class closest to your unit to auto-fill a typical multiplier range, or override it if your manufacturer publishes an actual figure.
This is a budgetary planning heuristic, not manufacturer inrush data or a time-domain simulation — it's not a substitute for the transformer's actual published inrush curve when sizing protective devices or generator/UPS ride-through.

BESS Sizing Calculator

Converts a critical load and backup duration into usable energy, then to the nameplate energy rating you'd need to procure once depth of discharge and round-trip efficiency losses are factored in. See Battery Storage for DC block configurations.

Usable kWh = kW × hours  ·  Nameplate kWh = Usable kWh / (DoD × RTE)

BESS Size

Defaults (90% DoD, 90% RTE) are typical for utility-scale LFP systems — adjust to your spec sheet.

Solar String Sizing Calculator

Applies module temperature coefficients to your site's design temperatures to find the maximum string length (cold-weather Voc must stay under the inverter's max input voltage) and minimum string length (hot-weather Vmp must stay in the inverter's MPPT window).

Voc(cold) = Voc × (1 + (Tc·Voc/100) × (Tmin−25))  ·  Vmp(hot) = Vmp × (1 + (Tc·Vmp/100) × (Tmax−25))

String Size

All values from the module and inverter datasheets — this tool does no lookups of its own.

Fault Current Estimate & Withstand Check

A simplified, infinite-source estimate of available symmetrical fault current at a transformer's secondary terminals — useful for a first-pass equipment rating check, not a substitute for a full short-circuit/coordination study that accounts for source and cable impedance.

Isc = (100,000 × kVA) / (1.732 × V × %Z)

Available Fault Current

%Z comes from the transformer nameplate — typical distribution transformers run 5.75%–6.5%, but always use the actual nameplate value. Common standard equipment AIC/withstand ratings: 10, 14, 18, 22, 25, 35, 42, 50, 65, 100, 150, 200 kA.

Motor Starting Voltage Dip

Estimates the voltage dip at a bus when a large motor starts across-the-line, using the standard "infinite bus minus source impedance" screening method behind IEEE Std 399 motor-starting studies — the motor's locked-rotor demand as a fraction of itself plus the source's short-circuit capacity. It's a snapshot of maximum dip only; it doesn't model acceleration time, other motors reaccelerating, or a limited-capacity generator source (which needs its transient reactance modeled separately, not this infinite-bus assumption).

%VoltageDip = LRkVA / (LRkVA + SCkVA) × 100

Voltage Dip on Motor Start

Use actual nameplate LRA when you have it — the NEMA code letter only gives a band, not an exact value. Use the system's minimum expected fault capacity (weakest condition), the opposite convention from a protective-device duty study.
Minimum acceptable starting-bus voltage is application-specific — commonly cited screening thresholds include ~80% of rated voltage at the starting motor's own terminals (NEMA Design B, full-torque load), ~85% for AC contactor pickup, and ~71% at the terminals of other motors on the same bus that need to reaccelerate. There's no single universal cutoff.

Arc-Flash Incident Energy Calculator

A first-pass estimate of arc-flash incident energy and the arc-flash protection boundary, using the theoretical Lee equation (open-air arcs above 600V) and the empirical Doughty equations (open-air or arc-in-a-box, 600V and below) — both documented in NFPA 70E Annex D. These are not the IEEE 1584 arcing-current method that a real arc-flash study or PPE label uses; they're simpler closed-form equations meant for early budgetary estimates, and they don't account for equipment-specific arcing current, electrode configuration, or enclosure size the way IEEE 1584 does. Always get a qualified arc-flash study for PPE selection and labeling.

E = 793 × F × V × t / D²  [Lee, open air, >600V]

Arc-Flash Incident Energy

Doughty equations (≤600V) are only validated for 16–50 kA bolted fault current and working distances of 18 in or more — the tool will flag it if you're outside that range.
Arc duration is your upstream protective device's actual clearing time (breaker + relay, from a coordination study) — there's no universal default, since it's the single biggest driver of incident energy. Bolted fault current can come from the Fault Current Estimate calculator above, or an actual short-circuit study. The 1.2 cal/cm² boundary threshold is the standard "just curable" second-degree-burn value from NFPA 70E Annex D — bump it to 1.5 cal/cm² if you're above 600V with a clearing time of 0.1s or less, per the same Annex D guidance.

Ground Rod Resistance Estimator

Estimates the resistance-to-earth of a single driven ground rod using Dwight's formula, the standard starting point for grounding-grid work referenced in IEEE Std 142. Real (layered, non-uniform) soil commonly deviates from this uniform-resistivity assumption by 10–20% or more — treat this as a first-pass design estimate, not a substitute for a field measurement (Wenner four-point method, IEEE Std 81) or a full grounding-grid study.

R = [ρ / (2πL)] × [ln(4L/d) − 1]

Single Rod Resistance

Soil resistivity presets are order-of-magnitude only (moisture, compaction, and mineral content routinely swing real resistivity by 10× within one "soil type") — a site resistivity test is the real number.
The commonly cited 25-ohm figure (NEC 250.53(A)(2)) is a pass/fail test for whether a single rod/pipe/plate electrode needs a supplemental electrode — it's enforceable rule text (an Exception), not just an informational note, and it doesn't apply once you're already using multiple electrodes. It's also not a general "the grounding system must measure 25Ω" requirement. Always confirm against the NEC edition your jurisdiction has adopted.

Transformer TCO Estimator

A transformer loses energy as heat continuously: a fixed no-load loss (core loss, present anytime it's energized, loaded or not) and a load loss (winding loss that scales with the square of the load factor). Over a 20–30 year service life, that loss can cost more than the transformer itself — so total cost of ownership, not sticker price, is often the better basis for comparing bids of the same kVA size. Enter both loss figures from the nameplate or factory test report.

Annual loss cost = [(Pnl × 8760) + (Pll × LoadFactor² × 8760)] × $/kWh

Transformer TCO

Loss figures come from the transformer's nameplate or factory test report — this tool does no lookups of its own.

MACRS Depreciation Estimator

MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System) lets US businesses depreciate equipment faster than its useful life, using the IRS's published 200%-declining-balance, half-year-convention percentage tables (Publication 946, Table A-1). Most energy equipment falls under 5-year or 7-year property.

MACRS Schedule

Not tax advice. This is the standard MACRS percentage table only — it doesn't account for bonus depreciation (many new energy assets qualify for significant first-year expensing), Section 179 expensing, or your specific tax situation. Confirm your actual depreciation schedule and eligibility with a qualified CPA.

Investment Payback & NPV Calculator

Simple payback and net present value for a BESS, solar, or efficiency-upgrade investment — how many years until annual savings/revenue cover the upfront cost, and what that cash flow is worth today once a discount rate is applied. Same NPV-of-a-level-annuity method as the Transformer TCO estimator above.

Simple payback (yrs) = Upfront cost / Annual savings  ·  NPV = Annual savings × [1 − (1+r)−n] / r − Upfront cost

Payback & NPV

"Annual savings/revenue" is your own estimate — energy arbitrage, demand-charge reduction, incentive payments, avoided utility spend, whatever applies. This assumes a level (non-escalating) annual figure.
Simple payback ignores the time value of money and stops counting once the analysis period is over — a project that pays back in year 12 of a 15-year analysis still needs the discount rate/NPV figure to judge whether it was worth doing, not just the payback year alone. This also doesn't model financing costs, tax credits/depreciation (see the MACRS estimator above), or savings escalation over time.

External Data & Price Trackers

Copper/aluminum pricing and interconnection queue congestion both move fast enough that a static number on this page would be stale within days — so rather than publish figures we can't keep current, here are the real sources procurement and interconnection teams actually track:

  • LME Copper and LME Aluminium — London Metal Exchange official daily reference prices, the standard benchmark for copper and aluminum used in cable, bus, and transformer winding cost.
  • EIA Electricity Data — U.S. Energy Information Administration's industrial and commercial electricity price data by state, useful as an input to the TCO calculator above.
  • Berkeley Lab "Queued Up" — Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's annual survey of US interconnection queues, aggregating data from ISOs/RTOs and utility balancing areas nationwide.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate voltage drop for a cable run?

Using the K-factor method: VD = (2 × K × I × D) / CM for single-phase or VD = (1.732 × K × I × D) / CM for three-phase, where K is a resistivity constant (about 12.9 for copper, 21.2 for aluminum), I is load current in amps, D is one-way distance in feet, and CM is the conductor's circular mil area. This is an approximation that ignores reactance and is best suited to feeders under about 250 feet.

What size transformer do I need for my load?

Divide your real load in kW by its power factor to get required kVA, then round up to the nearest standard transformer nameplate rating (common ANSI/IEEE sizes include 75, 112.5, 150, 225, 300, 500, 750, and 1000 kVA). Always leave margin for future load growth.

How do I size a battery energy storage system (BESS)?

Multiply the critical load in kW by the required backup duration in hours to get usable energy in kWh, then divide by your assumed depth of discharge and round-trip efficiency (commonly around 90% each for LFP systems) to get the nameplate energy rating you need to procure.

Are these calculators a substitute for a stamped engineering study?

No. These tools use standard simplified formulas for early-stage budgetary sizing and procurement planning. Final designs, especially fault current and arc-flash coordination, require a licensed engineer's study using actual system impedances.

How do you estimate arc-flash incident energy?

The theoretical Lee equation (NFPA 70E Annex D, for open-air arcs above 600V) and the empirical Doughty equations (for open-air or in-a-box arcs at 600V or below) estimate incident energy in cal/cm² from the bolted fault current, system voltage, arc duration, and working distance. These are simplified, standard-referenced methods for early-stage estimates — a real arc-flash study for PPE selection and labeling should use the full IEEE 1584 arcing-current method performed by a qualified person.

How do you calculate the total cost of ownership of a transformer?

Add the purchase price to the lifetime cost of its energy losses. No-load loss (core loss) runs continuously whenever the transformer is energized; load loss (winding loss) scales with the square of the load factor. Multiply each by 8760 hours/year and your electricity rate to get an annual loss cost, then extend it over the transformer's service life.

What is MACRS depreciation and how is it calculated for energy equipment?

MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System) lets businesses depreciate equipment faster than its useful life using IRS-published percentage tables (5-year and 7-year property are common for energy equipment). This is a simplified estimate only — it doesn't account for bonus depreciation or Section 179 expensing, and isn't tax advice.

How do you calculate the landed or delivered cost of imported equipment?

Add the equipment price, freight, duty (equipment price multiplied by the applicable duty rate), and cargo insurance together. The duty rate depends on the item's actual customs classification, which should come from a licensed customs broker, not an estimate.

How do you estimate how long a generator will run on a full tank?

Divide the fuel tank capacity (gallons) by the fuel consumption rate at your expected load (gallons per hour), taken from the generator's own spec sheet — most gensets publish consumption rates at 50%, 75%, and 100% load.
