Chilled Water Flow Calculator

Calculate chilled water flow in GPM from cooling load in tons or kW and the supply-return temperature difference.

Chilled Water Flow

GPM = BTU/h ÷ (500 × ΔT). Plain water.

Chilled water flow in GPM is the load in BTU per hour divided by 500 times the temperature difference in degrees Fahrenheit. With the load in tons it shortens to GPM = tons × 24 / ΔT. At the traditional 10°F difference that is 2.4 GPM per ton; at 12°F it is 2.0 and at 16°F it is 1.5.

The temperature difference is the lever. A wider one moves the same heat with less water, which means smaller pipes and less pump energy. It is why chilled water systems for data centers are designed with wider differences than the old comfort-cooling rule of thumb.

The formula

GPM = BTU/h / (500 × ΔT)
GPM = tons × 24 / ΔT
GPMWater flow in US gallons per minute
ΔTDifference between return and supply water temperature, in °F
500A constant for water: 8.33 lb/gal × 60 minutes × specific heat 1.0 BTU/lb·°F
tonsCooling load in tons of refrigeration, 12,000 BTU/h each

The equipment

Worked example

A 100 ton load with water supplied at 44°F and returning at 54°F:

GPM = 100 × 24 / 10 = 240 GPM

Widen the difference to 16°F and the same load needs 150 GPM. Because pump power falls much faster than flow, the saving in pumping energy is larger than the 38 percent cut in flow suggests.

A 500 kW IT load is 500 × 3,412 = 1,706,000 BTU/h, or 142 tons. At a 12°F difference it needs 284 GPM.

Which standard governs this

The equation is the standard hydronic heat-transfer relation from the ASHRAE Handbook. Chiller ratings are certified under AHRI Standard 550/590, which is where the traditional 44°F leaving water and 2.4 GPM per ton rating condition comes from.

What this calculation does not account for

The constant of 500 is for plain water at typical chilled water temperatures. A glycol mix has a lower specific heat and a higher density, so it needs more flow for the same load: roughly 5 to 15 percent more depending on the type and concentration. The result is the flow at the design load. It does not tell you the temperature difference the coils will actually achieve, and a system that returns water colder than designed needs more flow than this.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Using 2.4 GPM per ton on a system designed for a wider temperature difference. Applying the water constant to a glycol system. Sizing the pump for design flow and ignoring that low return temperature will ask for more. Mixing up tons of cooling at the coil with the electrical kW of the IT load without converting.
