Battery Runtime Calculator

Estimate how long a battery will carry a load from its amp-hours, voltage, usable depth of discharge and inverter efficiency.

Battery Runtime

Usable energy over load. Reliable for discharges of several hours; optimistic for fast discharges, and not a substitute for UPS battery tables.

Runtime is usable battery energy divided by the load. Usable energy is amp-hours times voltage, reduced by how deeply the battery may be discharged and by what the inverter loses on the way to the load. A 100 Ah, 48 V battery holds 4,800 Wh on the nameplate and delivers noticeably less than that to an AC load.

The estimate is dependable when the discharge is slow, over several hours. It becomes optimistic as the discharge gets faster, and for a UPS that empties its battery in ten or fifteen minutes it should not be used at all. The calculator flags the discharge rate so you can tell which case you are in.

The formula

Usable energy (Wh) = Ah × V × depth of discharge × efficiency
Runtime (h) = usable energy / load (W)
AhRated capacity of the battery in amp-hours
VNominal battery voltage
Depth of dischargeThe fraction of capacity that may be used, as a percentage
EfficiencyInverter and wiring efficiency between battery and load, as a percentage. Use 100 for a DC load fed directly
LoadPower drawn by the load, in watts

Worked example

A 100 Ah, 48 V battery, discharged to 80 percent depth through a 90 percent efficient inverter, carrying 1,000 W:

Usable energy = 100 × 48 × 0.80 × 0.90 = 3,456 Wh

Runtime = 3,456 / 1,000 = 3.46 hours, or about 3 h 27 min

The load is 1,000 W on a 4,800 Wh battery, a little over 0.2C. Raise the load to 4,800 W and the arithmetic says 43 minutes, but a lead-acid battery discharged at 1C delivers far less than its rated amp-hours, so the real figure would be shorter.

Which standard governs this

For stationary lead-acid batteries, IEEE 485 is the recommended practice for sizing against a duty cycle, and it works from the manufacturer's discharge tables with temperature, aging and design margins applied. IEEE 1184 covers battery selection for UPS systems. A calculation of this kind is the first estimate that comes before either.

What this calculation does not account for

Rated amp-hours apply at one discharge rate, commonly the 8, 10 or 20 hour rate for lead-acid. Discharged faster, a lead-acid battery gives up less charge, which this method ignores. It also leaves out temperature, the capacity lost with age, and the end-of-discharge voltage the equipment needs. UPS batteries are specified in watts per cell at a given runtime for exactly this reason, and a UPS runtime should come from those tables.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Using nameplate energy with no allowance for depth of discharge or inverter losses. Applying a slow-rate amp-hour rating to a fast discharge. Sizing for a new battery and forgetting it has to meet the runtime at end of life. Confusing the load in watts with the UPS rating in VA.
