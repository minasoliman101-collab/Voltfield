Electricity Cost Calculator

Work out what a load costs to run: kW, hours per day and the energy rate give kWh and cost per day, month and year.

Electricity Cost

Energy charge only. Demand charges, fixed charges and taxes are not included.

Energy cost is power times time times the rate: kW × hours × price per kWh. A 10 kW load running around the clock uses 240 kWh a day and 87,600 kWh a year. At $0.12 per kWh that is $10,512 a year.

For a load that runs continuously, a useful shortcut follows from the 8,760 hours in a year: each kilowatt costs 8,760 times the rate per year, which at $0.12 is about $1,051 per kW.

The formula

Energy (kWh) = load (kW) × hours of operation
Cost = energy (kWh) × rate (per kWh)
LoadAverage real power drawn while running, in kW
Hours per dayHours of operation in a day, up to 24
RateEnergy charge per kWh, from the tariff or the bill

Worked example

A 10 kW load running 24 hours a day at $0.12 per kWh:

Daily: 10 × 24 = 240 kWh, costing $28.80

Yearly: 240 × 365 = 87,600 kWh, costing $10,512

Monthly, taken as a twelfth of the year: 7,300 kWh, costing $876

The same load running 10 hours a day would use 36,500 kWh a year and cost $4,380.

Which standard governs this

No engineering standard governs this arithmetic. The rate, and what else appears on the bill, is set by the utility's tariff, and for a commercial or industrial account the tariff is where most of the difficulty lies.

What this calculation does not account for

This is the energy charge only. Commercial and industrial tariffs usually add a demand charge on the highest kW drawn in the billing period, which for a large steady load can rival the energy charge, and many add time-of-use rates, power factor penalties, fixed charges and taxes. It also assumes the load draws the same power for every hour it runs; use an average, not the nameplate, for equipment that cycles.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Using nameplate power for equipment that rarely runs at nameplate. Multiplying by a blended rate taken from one bill and applying it to a different load shape. Ignoring demand charges on a commercial account. Entering kVA where kW is needed, which overstates the energy by the power factor.
