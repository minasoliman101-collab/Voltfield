Airflow (CFM) Calculator

Airflow for a Heat Load

CFM = BTU/h ÷ (1.08 × ΔT). Sensible heat, standard air at sea level.m³/h = kW × 2,982 ÷ ΔT in kelvin. Sensible heat, standard air at sea level.

The airflow needed to carry away a heat load is the load in BTU per hour divided by 1.08 times the air temperature rise in degrees Fahrenheit. For 10 kW of IT equipment and a 20°F rise that is about 1,580 CFM, or 158 CFM per kW. Halve the temperature rise and the airflow doubles.

This is the sum behind every question about whether a rack, a row or a room has enough air. Almost all the electrical power going into IT equipment leaves as heat, so the heat load in kW is the electrical load in kW.

The airflow needed to carry away a heat load, in cubic metres per hour, is the load in kilowatts times 2,982, divided by the air temperature rise in kelvin. For 10 kW of IT equipment and an 11 K rise that is about 2,711 m³/h, or 271 m³/h per kW. Halve the temperature rise and the airflow doubles.

The formula

CFM = BTU/h / (1.08 × ΔT)
BTU/h = kW × 3,412
CFMAirflow in cubic feet per minute
ΔTTemperature rise of the air across the equipment, in °F
1.08A constant for standard air: density 0.075 lb/ft³ × specific heat 0.24 BTU/lb·°F × 60 minutes
kWSensible heat load, which for IT equipment is its electrical input
Q (m³/h) = P (kW) × 2,982 / ΔT (K)
Q (m³/s) = P (kW) / (1.2 × 1.006 × ΔT)
QAirflow, in cubic metres per hour or per second
ΔTTemperature rise of the air across the equipment, in kelvin. One kelvin is the same step as one degree Celsius
2,982A constant for standard air: 3,600 seconds divided by density 1.2 kg/m³ and specific heat 1.006 kJ/kg·K
PSensible heat load, which for IT equipment is its electrical input

The equipment

Worked example

A rack drawing 10 kW, with air entering at 75°F and leaving at 95°F:

BTU/h = 10 × 3,412 = 34,121

CFM = 34,121 / (1.08 × 20) = 1,580 CFM, or 158 CFM per kW

The same rack with a 30°F rise needs 1,053 CFM. Wider temperature rise is the reason high-density equipment can be cooled with less air than its power suggests, and it is also why hot-aisle temperatures have climbed.

A rack drawing 10 kW, with air entering at 24°C and leaving at 35°C:

Q = 10 × 2,982 / 11 = 2,711 m³/h, or 271 m³/h per kW

The same rack with a 17 K rise needs 1,754 m³/h. Wider temperature rise is the reason high-density equipment can be cooled with less air than its power suggests, and it is also why hot-aisle temperatures have climbed.

Which standard governs this

The equation is the sensible heat equation from the ASHRAE Handbook of Fundamentals. Inlet temperature limits for IT equipment come from the ASHRAE TC 9.9 thermal guidelines, which recommend 64 to 81°F (18 to 27°C) at the equipment inlet for the common air-cooled classes.

What this calculation does not account for

The 1.08 constant is for standard air at sea level. Air is thinner at altitude, so the same heat needs more volume; at 5,000 ft the constant is closer to 0.90. The calculation covers sensible heat only, and it gives the airflow the equipment needs, not what the cooling units must deliver. Bypass and recirculation mean the room supply has to be higher than the sum of the equipment airflows unless containment is good.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Using the room temperature difference in place of the rise across the equipment. Forgetting altitude. Assuming the cooling units only need to supply the equipment airflow, with no allowance for air that bypasses the racks. Treating a nameplate power figure as the heat load, when measured draw is usually well below it.
