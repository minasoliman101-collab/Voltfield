Watts to Amps Calculator

Convert watts to amps for DC, single-phase and three-phase circuits, with the power factor and square-root-of-three terms in the right places.

Watts to Amps

Steady-state current. Power factor is ignored on DC, where it has no meaning. A load quoted in VA is apparent power — use the kVA converter instead.

Watts to amps is the same calculation as kilowatts to amps without the factor of a thousand, and it is the form that comes up on small loads: a power supply, a heater, a rack of equipment quoted in watts rather than kW.

The DC case is the clean one — amps are simply watts over volts. On AC, power factor enters, and on three-phase the square root of three enters as well. Most of the wrong answers in this conversion come from applying the DC form to an AC circuit.

The formula

I = W / V [DC]
I = W / (V × PF) [single-phase AC]
I = W / (1.732 × V × PF) [three-phase AC]
WReal power in watts
VVoltage. Line-to-line for three-phase
PFPower factor, between 0 and 1. Unity for a resistive load such as a heater
IResulting current in amperes

Worked example

A 1500 W space heater on a 120 V circuit, purely resistive:

I = 1500 / (120 × 1.0) = 12.5 A

That is why a 1500 W heater is the practical limit on a 15 A branch circuit: at 12.5 A it sits right at the 80 percent continuous-load figure.

A 50 kW three-phase load at 480 V, power factor 0.95:

I = 50,000 / (1.732 × 480 × 0.95) = 63.3 A

Which standard governs this

The arithmetic is definitional. The limits around it are not: NEC 210.19(A) requires branch-circuit conductors to be sized at 125 percent of a continuous load, which is where the familiar 80 percent figure comes from, and NEC 210.23 governs what may be connected to a given branch-circuit rating.

What this calculation does not account for

A steady-state figure at the stated power factor. It does not apply the continuous-load factor, and it does not cover inrush on a motor, a transformer or a switch-mode supply, each of which draws far more than this for a short time. For a load quoted in VA rather than watts, this is the wrong form — VA is apparent power, so there is no power factor term.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Using the DC form on an AC circuit, which omits power factor. Assuming unity power factor on anything with a motor or a drive in it. Treating a VA rating as watts. Loading a branch circuit to the calculated current with no allowance for the 125 percent continuous-load requirement.
