Conduit Fill Calculator

Check conduit fill with mixed conductor sizes: the Chapter 9 Table 1 caps, what counts toward fill, the 60 percent nipple allowance, and why Annex C can disagree.

Conduit Fill

Handles mixed conductor sizes in one raceway, applies the right Chapter 9 Table 1 cap for the total count, and names the smallest listed size that fits. Conductor and raceway areas come from Chapter 9 Tables 4 and 5 — a curated subset, with direct entry for anything not listed.

Conduit fill is a ratio, and the arithmetic is the easy part. What makes it go wrong is the three questions around it: which percentage cap applies, which conductors count, and whether the answer has to match NEC Annex C.

The cap is not a single number. One conductor in a raceway may fill 53 percent of it, two conductors 31 percent, and more than two 40 percent. That ordering looks wrong until you see what it is for — the limit exists so conductors can be pulled without damaging the insulation and so heat can get out, and a single conductor has no neighbours to bind against while two round conductors in a round raceway waste the space between them.

The formula

Fill % = 100 × (total conductor area) / (raceway internal area)
Total conductor area = sum over each size of (count × area from Chapter 9 Table 5)
Cap = 53% for one conductor, 31% for two, 40% for more than two, 60% for a nipple of 24 in. or less
Raceway internal areaTotal internal area for the trade size and type, from Chapter 9 Table 4
Conductor areaApproximate area including insulation, from Chapter 9 Table 5. Not the conductor metal area
Conductor countEvery current-carrying conductor plus every equipment grounding and bonding conductor
NippleA raceway not exceeding 600 mm, or 24 in., between enclosures

Worked example

Three 12 AWG THHN phase conductors, one 12 AWG neutral and one 10 AWG equipment grounding conductor, in 3/4 inch EMT.

Conductor area: 4 × 0.0133 + 1 × 0.0211 = 0.0743 in².

The raceway is 0.533 in² total internal area. That is five conductors, which is more than two, so the cap is 40 percent: 0.533 × 0.40 = 0.2132 in².

Fill is 0.0743 / 0.533 = 13.9 percent, comfortably inside the cap. Note that the grounding conductor was counted, and that a mixed bundle cannot be checked by looking up either size on its own, because the cap depends on the total count rather than on any one size.

Which standard governs this

NEC Chapter 9, Table 1 gives the percentage caps and its notes carry most of the rules that get missed: equipment grounding and bonding conductors are included in the calculation, a multiconductor cable is treated as a single conductor, and a nipple not exceeding 24 in. may be filled to 60 percent. Table 4 gives raceway internal areas by type and trade size, and Table 5 gives approximate conductor areas including insulation. NEC Annex C publishes pre-computed conductor counts per raceway for the case where every conductor is the same size and type.

What this calculation does not account for

This is area arithmetic, and area arithmetic is not the whole of a raceway design. It says nothing about the number of bends between pull points under NEC 358.26 and its equivalents, nothing about pulling tension or lubrication, and nothing about ampacity: more than three current-carrying conductors in a raceway triggers the adjustment factors in NEC 310.15(C)(1), and a raceway that passes fill can still force the conductors up a size. The raceway and conductor areas offered here are a curated subset rather than a reproduction of Tables 4 and 5, so anything outside the list has to be entered from the tables directly. Results can differ from Annex C by about one conductor, because Annex C carries NFPA's own rounding conventions.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Leaving the equipment grounding conductor out of the count. Using the conductor metal area, or a figure from a wire gauge conversion, instead of the Table 5 area including insulation. Applying the 40 percent cap to one or two conductors, where the cap is actually higher and lower respectively. Checking a mixed bundle by looking up each size separately, when the cap depends on the total count. Treating a multiconductor cable as its individual conductors. Assuming a passing fill means the ampacity is fine. Using the 60 percent nipple allowance on a raceway longer than 24 in. Taking area-ratio math as authoritative where the jurisdiction expects Annex C.
