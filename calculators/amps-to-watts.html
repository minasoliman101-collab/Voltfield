Amps to Watts Calculator

Convert amps to watts for DC, single-phase and three-phase circuits, with power factor and the square root of three where they belong.

Amps to Watts

Real power at the stated power factor. Power factor is ignored on DC, where it has no meaning.

Watts are volts times amps on DC. On AC, multiply by power factor as well, and on three-phase by the square root of three. Amps alone do not give watts. The voltage has to be known, which is why 10 A can be 1,200 W on a 120 V circuit and 2,400 W on a 240 V one.

This is the conversion for a clamp-meter reading that has to become a power figure, or a nameplate current that has to be compared with a supply rated in watts.

The formula

W = V × I [DC]
W = V × I × PF [single-phase AC]
W = 1.732 × V × I × PF [three-phase AC]
ICurrent in amperes. Line current for three-phase
VVoltage. Line-to-line for three-phase
PFPower factor, between 0 and 1. Unity for a resistive load
WReal power in watts

Worked example

A resistive load drawing 12.5 A on a 120 V circuit:

W = 120 × 12.5 × 1.0 = 1,500 W

A three-phase motor load drawing 63.3 A at 480 V with a power factor of 0.95:

W = 1.732 × 480 × 63.3 × 0.95 = 49,995 W, or about 50 kW

Drop the power factor from the second case and the answer is 52.6 kVA. That figure is apparent power, and it overstates the real power by 5 percent.

Which standard governs this

The arithmetic is definitional. Where the result is used to load a circuit, NEC 210.19(A) and 210.20(A) require conductors and overcurrent devices to be sized at 125 percent of a continuous load.

What this calculation does not account for

This is real power at one operating point, at the power factor you enter. It does not know the power factor of your load; if you leave it at 1.0 for a motor or a drive, the answer is volt-amperes labelled as watts. The three-phase form assumes balanced phases.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Treating volts times amps as watts on an AC load with a power factor below one. Leaving out the square root of three on three-phase. Using a current measured on one phase of an unbalanced system as though it applied to all three. Quoting a wattage from amps without saying at what voltage.
