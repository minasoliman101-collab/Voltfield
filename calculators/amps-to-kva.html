Amps to kVA Calculator

Convert amps to kVA for single-phase and three-phase systems. Apparent power is volts times amps, with no power factor term.

Amps to kVA

Apparent power from line current and voltage. Use line-to-line voltage for three-phase.

kVA is volts times amps divided by a thousand, with a square root of three added for three-phase. There is no power factor in it. Apparent power is what the current and voltage amount to together, whether or not all of it does useful work, and it is what transformers, generators and UPS systems are rated in.

The usual reason to run this conversion is a measured or nameplate current that has to be compared with a kVA rating: whether a 100 A load fits on a 75 kVA transformer, or how much of a generator a panel is using.

The formula

kVA = V × I / 1000 [single-phase]
kVA = 1.732 × V × I / 1000 [three-phase]
VVoltage. Line-to-line for three-phase
ILine current in amperes
kVAApparent power in kilovolt-amperes

Worked example

A three-phase panel draws 100 A per phase at 480 V:

kVA = 1.732 × 480 × 100 / 1000 = 83.1 kVA

That is more than a 75 kVA transformer is rated for, so the next standard three-phase size, 112.5 kVA, is the one that fits.

A single-phase 240 V service drawing 200 A:

kVA = 240 × 200 / 1000 = 48 kVA

Which standard governs this

The arithmetic is definitional. Transformer kVA ratings and the conditions they apply under are set by the IEEE C57 series, and the NEC sizes transformer protection from rated current under Article 450.

What this calculation does not account for

The three-phase form assumes a balanced load, with the same current in each phase. On an unbalanced system, use the highest phase current if the question is whether equipment is overloaded. The result is apparent power only. To get real power in kW, multiply by power factor.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Leaving out the square root of three on a three-phase system, which understates kVA by 42 percent. Using line-to-neutral voltage in the three-phase formula. Multiplying by power factor, which gives kW, not kVA. Comparing the result with a kW rating as though the two were the same unit.
