Three-Phase Power Calculator

Solve for voltage, current, power or power factor in a balanced three-phase system, with line and phase values for delta and wye.

Three-Phase Power

Enter any three of line voltage, line current, real power and power factor; the fourth follows. Reports line and phase values for both delta and wye, since that is where the factor of √3 actually goes.

Almost every three-phase mistake is a factor of √3 in the wrong place, and the reason is that the formula people memorise hides where it belongs.

Power in a balanced three-phase system is √3 × line voltage × line current × power factor. The √3 is there because line voltage and phase voltage are not the same thing, and which one differs depends on how the windings are connected. In a wye, line voltage is √3 times phase voltage and the line current passes straight through one winding, so line and phase current are equal. In a delta it reverses: each winding sees full line voltage, while line current splits between two windings, so line current is √3 times winding current.

Nameplates and meters give line values. The phase values are what you need when you are working inside a winding, checking one leg, or reading a current transformer.

The formula

kW = √3 × Vline × Iline × PF / 1000
kVA = √3 × Vline × Iline / 1000
Wye: Vphase = Vline / √3, Iphase = Iline
Delta: Vphase = Vline, Iphase = Iline / √3
V lineLine-to-line voltage. The figure on the nameplate and on the drawing
I lineCurrent in one of the three supply conductors
PFPower factor of the load, between 0 and 1
√31.732, from the 120-degree phase displacement between the three voltages

Worked example

A motor on a 480 V three-phase supply draws 100 A at 0.85 power factor.

kVA = 1.732 × 480 × 100 / 1000 = 83.1 kVA.

kW = 83.1 × 0.85 = 70.6 kW of real power, with 43.8 kvar reactive.

Now the part that gets missed. If that load is wye-connected, each winding sees 480 / 1.732 = 277 V and carries the full 100 A. If it is delta-connected, each winding sees the full 480 V and carries 100 / 1.732 = 57.7 A. The supply is identical in both cases and the line measurements are identical; only the internal stresses differ.

Which standard governs this

This is circuit theory rather than a code requirement, and it holds for any balanced three-phase system at a single frequency. The voltage designations used in North America, such as 208Y/120, 480Y/277 and 240 delta, come from ANSI C84.1, which also sets the permitted voltage ranges at the service and at the point of utilization. Conductor sizing from the resulting current follows NEC 310 and, for motors specifically, Article 430, which requires the table full-load current rather than measured load.

What this calculation does not account for

Everything here assumes a balanced load, meaning equal current in all three phases. On an unbalanced system the single-figure answer stops being meaningful: real power has to be summed per phase, and a neutral current appears that the balanced formula does not predict. It also assumes reasonably sinusoidal current, so a heavily non-linear load breaks the simple power-factor relationship, and it says nothing about which direction reactive power flows. The connection shown is the load's, which is not necessarily the transformer secondary's.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Applying √3 to a single-phase circuit, or omitting it from a three-phase one. Using phase voltage where the formula wants line voltage, which on a 480Y/277 system gives an answer low by a factor of 1.732. Dividing three-phase power by three to get per-phase power and then applying √3 again. Treating a measured line current as the winding current in a delta load. Using the balanced formula on a visibly unbalanced panel. Reading kVA as kW, which only match at unity power factor.
