BTU to Tons Converter

Convert BTU/h, tons of refrigeration and kW, including IT load to cooling tons. What a ton actually measures and what the figure leaves out.

BTU/h, Tons and kW

Converts between the three cooling-capacity units. For an IT load, enter the electrical kW — essentially all of it becomes heat, so the thermal load is the same number.

A ton of refrigeration is not a weight, and nothing about the unit makes that obvious. It is a rate of heat removal, defined as the rate that would melt a short ton of ice over 24 hours. That definition fixes it at exactly 12,000 BTU per hour, or about 3.517 kW.

The conversions are therefore exact and uninteresting. What makes the question worth a page is the one most people are actually asking: how much cooling does a given electrical load need. For IT equipment the answer is unintuitively simple, because essentially all the electrical energy going into a server leaves it as heat. A 100 kW IT load is a 100 kW thermal load, so the problem reduces to a unit conversion rather than an energy balance.

The formula

Tons = BTU/h / 12,000
kW (thermal) = tons × 3.517
BTU/h = kW × 3,412
IT load: thermal kW ≈ electrical kW
Ton of refrigeration12,000 BTU/h by definition. A rate, not a mass
BTU/hBritish thermal units per hour, the conventional HVAC capacity unit in North America
kW thermalHeat removal rate in kilowatts. Not the power the chiller draws
Spare capacityHeadroom added on top of the calculated load, as a percentage

Worked example

A data hall carries 250 kW of IT load.

Essentially all of it becomes heat, so the thermal load is 250 kW.

250 / 3.517 = 71.1 tons, which is 853,000 BTU/h.

Adding 20 percent spare gives 85.3 tons, or 300 kW of cooling capacity.

Note what that figure is not. It is the heat the plant has to remove, not the electricity the plant consumes doing it, and not the capacity the facility needs once redundancy is applied. A room designed to lose no cooling on a single failure needs more installed capacity than 85 tons, arranged so that the remaining units still cover the load.

Which standard governs this

The ton of refrigeration is a conventional unit rather than an SI one, fixed at 12,000 BTU/h. Equipment capacity ratings and the conditions they are measured at come from AHRI certification standards, which matters because a nominal tonnage is stated at specific entering and ambient conditions and real capacity falls away from them. ASHRAE publishes the thermal guidelines that set the temperature and humidity envelopes data center equipment is expected to operate within, which determine what the cooling system has to achieve rather than how much capacity it needs.

What this calculation does not account for

This converts capacity units and nothing else. It does not size a cooling system: it ignores the latent load from humidity control, the outside-air and envelope loads in a mixed-use space, the power the cooling plant itself draws, and the redundancy the facility is designed to, all of which sit between a heat load and an equipment schedule. Nominal equipment tonnage is rated at standard conditions and the delivered capacity differs at real entering water and ambient air temperatures. The IT assumption that electrical input equals thermal output is very close but not exact, and it does not hold for loads that do external work, such as motors driving something outside the space.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Reading a ton as a weight or as a size of unit rather than a rate of heat removal. Confusing cooling capacity in kW with the electrical power the chiller draws, which differ by the system's efficiency. Sizing cooling from a UPS or transformer rating rather than the actual IT load, which inflates the figure by whatever the equipment is oversized by. Taking nominal tonnage as available capacity at design-day conditions. Adding spare capacity and redundancy as if they were the same thing. Applying the electrical-equals-thermal shortcut to motor loads doing work outside the room.
