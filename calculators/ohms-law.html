Ohm's Law Calculator

Ohm's Law

Fill in any two and leave the other two blank. For DC and for resistive AC loads.

Ohm's law says voltage equals current times resistance: V = I × R. Add the power relation, P = V × I, and any two of the four quantities fix the other two. That is all this calculator does: give it two, and it returns the missing pair and tells you which form of the equations it used.

It is the right tool for a resistive load on DC, and for a resistive load on AC such as a heater or an incandescent lamp. It is the wrong tool for anything with a motor, a transformer or a power supply in it, where current and voltage are out of step and power factor has to be included.

The formula

V = I × R
P = V × I = I² × R = V² / R
VVoltage across the load, in volts
ICurrent through the load, in amperes
RResistance of the load, in ohms
PPower dissipated in the load, in watts

Worked example

A load draws 10 A from a 120 V supply:

R = 120 / 10 = 12 Ω, and P = 120 × 10 = 1,200 W.

Working the other way, a 1,500 W heater on 120 V:

I = 1,500 / 120 = 12.5 A, and R = 120² / 1,500 = 9.6 Ω.

The second form explains why a heating element run at half its rated voltage gives a quarter of its rated heat. Resistance stays the same, and power goes with the square of the voltage.

Which standard governs this

Ohm's law is a physical relationship, not a code requirement, so no standard governs the arithmetic. What it feeds into is governed: conductor sizing under NEC Article 310 and overcurrent protection under Article 240 both start from the current this kind of calculation produces.

What this calculation does not account for

The resistance of a real conductor or heating element changes with temperature, so a figure measured cold is lower than the figure at operating temperature. On AC, this form holds only for a purely resistive load. Motors, transformers, drives and power supplies have reactance, and for those the relationship is between voltage, current and impedance, with power factor in the power equation.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Entering three values that do not agree with each other and trusting whichever answer comes out. Using P = V × I for an AC load with a power factor below one, which gives volt-amperes, not watts. Using the cold resistance of a lamp or element to predict its running current. Forgetting that halving the voltage quarters the power.
