Wire Resistance Calculator

Find the resistance of a copper or aluminum conductor from its size and length, one way or out and back, in ohms and ohms per 1000 ft.

Find the resistance of a copper or aluminum conductor from its size and length, one way or out and back, in ohms and ohms per km.

Wire Resistance

K = 12.9 (copper) or 21.2 (aluminum) ohm·cmil/ft. DC resistance at about 75°C.ρ = 0.0214 (copper) or 0.0352 (aluminum) Ω·mm²/m. DC resistance at about 75°C.

A conductor's resistance is its length divided by its cross-sectional area, times a constant for the metal. Double the length and resistance doubles. Go up three AWG sizes and the area doubles, so resistance halves. Aluminum has about 1.6 times the resistance of copper at the same size.

Resistance is what voltage drop and conductor heating come from, so this is the figure underneath both. For a two-wire circuit the current flows out and back, and the resistance that matters is that of both conductors, twice the one-way figure.

The formula

R = K × L / A
RResistance in ohms
K12.9 for copper, 21.2 for aluminum, in ohm-circular mils per foot at about 75°C
LLength in feet. One way, or doubled for a two-wire circuit
AConductor area in circular mils. 12 AWG is 6,530; 4/0 AWG is 211,600
R = ρ × L / A
RResistance in ohms
ρ0.0214 for copper, 0.0352 for aluminum, in Ω·mm²/m at about 75°C

Worked example

100 ft of 12 AWG copper, one way:

R = 12.9 × 100 / 6,530 = 0.1975 Ω

That is 1.975 Ω per 1000 ft, in line with the figures NEC Chapter 9, Table 8 gives for 12 AWG copper.

For a two-wire circuit the current passes through 200 ft of conductor, so the resistance is 0.3951 Ω. At 16 A that drops 16 × 0.3951 = 6.3 V, which on a 120 V circuit is 5.3 percent, well past the 3 percent usually recommended for a branch circuit.

30 m of 12 AWG copper, one way:

R = 0.0214 × 30 / 3.31 = 0.1944 Ω

That is 6.48 Ω per km.

For a two-wire circuit the current passes through 60 m of conductor, so the resistance is 0.3889 Ω. At 16 A that drops 16 × 0.3889 = 6.2 V, which on a 120 V circuit is 5.2 percent, well past the 3 percent usually recommended for a branch circuit.

Which standard governs this

NEC Chapter 9, Table 8 gives DC resistance for conductors at 75°C, and Table 9 gives AC resistance and reactance for conductors in conduit. Conductor areas in circular mils follow the AWG definition in ASTM B258. The K-factor method used here is an approximation to Table 8.

What this calculation does not account for

This is DC resistance at one temperature. Resistance rises with temperature, by roughly 0.4 percent per degree Celsius for copper and aluminum, so a conductor running cooler than 75°C has less. On AC, skin effect and the conduit material raise the effective resistance of large conductors above the DC figure, and reactance adds to the impedance; for sizes above about 4/0, use NEC Table 9. It does not include the resistance of terminations and splices.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Using the one-way length for a two-wire circuit. Using copper figures for aluminum conductors. Expecting resistance to halve with each AWG size, when it takes three sizes. Ignoring temperature when comparing a calculated figure with a measurement taken on a cold cable.
