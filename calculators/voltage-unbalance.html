Voltage Unbalance Calculator

Calculate three-phase voltage unbalance from three line-to-line readings by the NEMA method, with the effect on motor heating.

Voltage Unbalance

NEMA method: largest deviation from the average of the three line-to-line voltages, over that average.

Voltage unbalance, by the NEMA definition, is the largest difference between any one line-to-line voltage and the average of the three, divided by that average. Readings of 460, 467 and 450 V average 459 V; the furthest from the average is 450 V, 9 V away, so the unbalance is 1.96 percent.

A small unbalance in voltage produces a much larger unbalance in motor current, commonly six to ten times as large in percentage terms, and the extra current turns into heat in the windings. This is why a figure that looks trivial is taken seriously.

The formula

Unbalance (%) = maximum deviation from average / average × 100
AverageThe mean of the three line-to-line voltages
Maximum deviationThe largest difference between any one reading and that average

The equipment

Worked example

Line-to-line readings of 460 V, 467 V and 450 V:

Average = (460 + 467 + 450) / 3 = 459 V

Deviations are 1 V, 8 V and 9 V, so the maximum is 9 V

Unbalance = 9 / 459 × 100 = 1.96%

A common rule of thumb puts the added winding temperature rise at about twice the square of the percentage unbalance: 2 × 1.96² is roughly 8 percent more temperature rise at full load.

Which standard governs this

NEMA MG 1 defines this method and states that motors are designed to operate at rated load with up to 1 percent voltage unbalance. Above that it gives a derating curve, and it does not recommend operation above 5 percent. ANSI C84.1 recommends that utility systems be designed to limit unbalance to 3 percent at no load. IEC standards define unbalance differently, from the ratio of negative to positive sequence voltage, and the two methods give similar but not identical figures.

What this calculation does not account for

The NEMA method uses magnitudes only and ignores phase angle, so it is an approximation to the true sequence-component unbalance. It is taken from line-to-line voltages; using line-to-neutral readings gives a different and usually larger number. Readings should be taken at the motor terminals with the motor running, because unbalance caused by a poor connection only appears under load. The temperature-rise rule is an estimate, and the derating a specific motor needs comes from the NEMA curve or its manufacturer.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Dividing the spread between highest and lowest reading by the average, which overstates the result. Using line-to-neutral voltages. Measuring at the switchboard and not at the motor. Chasing an unbalance in the supply when the cause is a single-phase load, a blown capacitor fuse or a high-resistance connection downstream.
