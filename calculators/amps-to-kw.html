Amps to kW Calculator

Convert amps to kilowatts for single-phase, three-phase and DC circuits, with the power factor term that makes the difference.

Amps to kW

Real power at the operating point you enter. Power factor is ignored on DC, where it has no meaning.

Amps measure current. Kilowatts measure real power — the part actually doing work. Converting between them needs the voltage, and on an AC circuit it also needs the power factor, because current and voltage are not in step. Leave power factor out and the answer comes back high by however far the load sits from unity, which on a motor-heavy feeder is 10 to 20 percent.

The three-phase case carries an extra factor of the square root of three. That term is not a fudge: it falls out of three voltages 120 degrees apart, and it is the single most commonly dropped part of this calculation.

The formula

kW = (V × I × PF) / 1000 [single-phase]
kW = (1.732 × V × I × PF) / 1000 [three-phase]
kW = (V × I) / 1000 [DC]
VVoltage. Line-to-line for three-phase, line-to-neutral for single-phase
ICurrent in amperes
PFPower factor, between 0 and 1. Unity on a purely resistive load; roughly 0.8 to 0.9 on a motor
1.732The square root of three, which appears in every three-phase power calculation

Worked example

A 100 A three-phase load at 480 V with a power factor of 0.9:

kW = (1.732 × 480 × 100 × 0.9) / 1000 = 74.8 kW

The same 100 A single-phase at 240 V, purely resistive:

kW = (240 × 100 × 1.0) / 1000 = 24 kW

Same current, very different power. The voltage and the number of phases do most of the work here.

Which standard governs this

There is no code section governing the arithmetic; it is the definition of real power. The place a standard enters is the current you put in: NEC Article 220 covers how load current is calculated for sizing purposes, and NEC 430.250 gives the full-load current tables for motors rather than letting you derive them.

What this calculation does not account for

This returns real power at the operating point you describe. It is not a demand figure, and it says nothing about what happens while a motor starts, where current can run six to eight times full-load for a few seconds. On a non-linear load — drives, rectifiers, switch-mode supplies — a single power factor also hides the distortion component, and the true power factor is lower than the displacement figure a meter reports.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Dropping the square root of three on a three-phase circuit, which overstates power by 73 percent. Using unity power factor because the real figure is unknown, which overstates it again. Mixing line-to-line and line-to-neutral voltage on the same calculation. Reading nameplate amps as operating amps: a nameplate states full-load current, and most equipment does not run there.
