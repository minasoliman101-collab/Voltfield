Pipe Velocity Calculator

Pipe Velocity

v = 0.4085 × GPM ÷ d². Use the inside diameter, not the nominal size: 4 in Schedule 40 steel is 4.026 in.v = 1,273 × L/s ÷ d², with d in millimetres. Use the inside diameter, not the nominal size: DN 100 Schedule 40 steel is 102.3 mm.

Water velocity in feet per second is 0.4085 times the flow in GPM, divided by the square of the pipe's inside diameter in inches. Because diameter is squared, one pipe size makes a large difference: the same 240 GPM runs at 6.0 ft/s in 4 inch pipe and 10.4 ft/s in 3 inch.

Velocity is the quick test of whether a pipe is the right size. Too fast, and the pipe is noisy, erodes at elbows and tees, and loses a great deal of pressure. Too slow, and air and dirt drop out of the flow instead of being carried to where they can be removed.

Water velocity in metres per second is 1,273 times the flow in litres per second, divided by the square of the pipe's inside diameter in millimetres. Because diameter is squared, one pipe size makes a large difference: the same 15 L/s runs at 1.8 m/s in DN 100 pipe and 3.1 m/s in DN 80.

The equipment

Worked example

240 GPM in 4 inch Schedule 40 steel pipe, which has an inside diameter of 4.026 in:

v = 0.4085 × 240 / 4.026² = 98.04 / 16.21 = 6.05 ft/s

The same flow in 3 inch Schedule 40, inside diameter 3.068 in, runs at 10.4 ft/s, which is on the fast side for a closed hydronic system.

15 L/s in DN 100 (4 inch) Schedule 40 steel pipe, which has an inside diameter of 102.3 mm:

v = 1,273 × 15 / 102.3² = 19,095 / 10,465 = 1.82 m/s

The same flow in DN 80 (3 inch) Schedule 40, inside diameter 77.9 mm, runs at 3.15 m/s, which is on the fast side for a closed hydronic system.

Which standard governs this

The equation is continuity: flow equals velocity times area. Pipe dimensions come from ASME B36.10 for steel pipe. The ASHRAE Handbook gives the customary hydronic sizing limits, commonly quoted as about 4 ft/s in pipe of 2 inches and under and a friction loss of about 4 ft of head per 100 ft in larger pipe.

What this calculation does not account for

Velocity alone does not give pressure drop, which also depends on pipe roughness, fluid viscosity and every fitting in the run. The limits quoted are design conventions for closed hydronic systems and are not requirements; domestic water, condenser water and process piping follow their own. The inside diameter changes with schedule and material, so the same nominal size gives different velocities in steel, copper and plastic.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Entering the nominal pipe size as the diameter. Using Schedule 40 dimensions for Schedule 80 or for copper tube. Judging a pipe on velocity and never checking the pressure drop. Sizing every pipe to the same velocity regardless of diameter, which makes small pipes noisy and large ones needlessly big.
