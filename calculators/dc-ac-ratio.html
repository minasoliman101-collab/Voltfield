DC/AC Ratio Calculator

Calculate a solar plant's DC/AC ratio from array DC capacity and inverter AC rating, and see how much the inverter would clip at full output.

DC/AC Ratio

Array DC nameplate over inverter AC rating. Also called the inverter loading ratio.

The DC/AC ratio is the array's DC nameplate capacity divided by the inverter's AC rating. It is also called the inverter loading ratio. A 130 kW array on a 100 kW inverter has a ratio of 1.30.

A ratio above 1.0 is deliberate. An array reaches its nameplate output only under standard test conditions, which it rarely sees in the field: modules run hot, the sun is seldom square to them, and wiring and soiling take their share. Oversizing the array fills more of the inverter's capacity for more hours of the day. The price is clipping, where the inverter caps its output during the hours the array could produce more.

The formula

DC/AC ratio = array DC capacity (kWp) / inverter AC rating (kW)
Array DC capacity = number of modules × module rating (W) / 1000
Array DC capacitySum of module nameplate ratings at standard test conditions, in kWp
Inverter AC ratingRated continuous AC output of the inverter, in kW

Worked example

A string inverter rated 100 kW AC is fed by 240 modules of 540 W each:

Array DC capacity = 240 × 540 / 1000 = 129.6 kWp

DC/AC ratio = 129.6 / 100 = 1.30

If the array ever did reach nameplate, it would offer the inverter 30 percent more than it can pass, and the excess would be clipped. In practice that happens on a limited number of cool, clear hours, which is why a ratio in the region of 1.2 to 1.3 is a common design choice.

Which standard governs this

The ratio itself is a design figure, not a code limit. The limits that bound it are electrical: NEC 690.7 sets the maximum DC voltage, NEC 690.8 the circuit currents, and the inverter data sheet gives the maximum DC power and short-circuit current its input will accept. An array can satisfy the ratio a developer wants and still exceed one of those.

What this calculation does not account for

The ratio does not tell you how much energy is clipped over a year. That depends on the site's irradiance, temperature, tilt and tracking, and it takes an hourly production model to estimate. It is also unaffected by module degradation, which lowers the effective ratio as the plant ages, and it says nothing about whether the string voltage and current fit the inverter's input window.

This is a screening estimate. It is here to get you to the right order of magnitude and the right conversation — not to replace a stamped calculation by a qualified engineer.

Common mistakes

Dividing AC by DC and reporting the inverse. Using the interconnection limit in place of the inverter rating without saying so. Treating the clipping percentage at nameplate as the annual energy loss, which is far smaller. Choosing a ratio from a rule of thumb for a different climate or a different mounting system.
