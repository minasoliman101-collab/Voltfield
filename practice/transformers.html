Transformers: Practice Questions

Full-load current, impedance and fault current, sizing from load, and nameplate terms.

Full-load current and available fault current are the two transformer calculations that come up most, and the second follows directly from the first. The later questions cover terms found on every nameplate.

Question 1

What is the full-load secondary current of a 500 kVA three-phase transformer at 480 V?

Choices for question 1
Show the worked solution

Answer: C. 601 A

I = kVA × 1,000 / (1.732 × V) = 500,000 / (1.732 × 480) = 601 A.

Related: kVA to Amps Calculator

Question 2

A 1,500 kVA, 480 V three-phase transformer has an impedance of 5.75 percent. Assuming an unlimited supply on the primary, what fault current is available at its secondary terminals?

Choices for question 2
Show the worked solution

Answer: A. 31,378 A

Full-load current = 1,500,000 / (1.732 × 480) = 1,804 A. Fault current = full-load current / (%Z / 100) = 1,804 / 0.0575 = 31,378 A, about 31.4 kA. Multiplying by the impedance, or leaving out the 1.732, gives the wrong choices.

Related: Fault Current Calculator

Question 3

A three-phase load of 300 kW has a power factor of 0.9. Which standard transformer size is the smallest that will carry it?

Choices for question 3
Show the worked solution

Answer: A. 500 kVA

kVA = kW / PF = 300 / 0.9 = 333 kVA. A 300 kVA unit is too small, and the next standard three-phase size above it is 500 kVA. Sizing on kW alone, and choosing 300 kVA, is the mistake the question is built around.

Related: Transformer Sizing Calculator

Question 4

A 75 kVA three-phase transformer steps 480 V down to 208 V. What is its full-load secondary current?

Choices for question 4
Show the worked solution

Answer: D. 208 A

On the 208 V side, I = 75,000 / (1.732 × 208) = 208 A. The primary carries 90 A. The kVA is the same on both sides; the current is not.

Related: kVA to Amps Chart

Question 5

Two transformers have the same kVA and voltage. One has 4 percent impedance and the other 6 percent. Which statement is correct?

Choices for question 5
Show the worked solution

Answer: B. The 4 percent unit delivers more fault current and has better voltage regulation.

Fault current is full-load current divided by the impedance as a fraction, so lower impedance means more fault current: 25 times full load at 4 percent against about 17 times at 6 percent. Lower impedance also means less voltage drop under load.

Related: Glossary: Transformer Impedance

Question 6

A transformer nameplate shows the vector group Dyn11. What does it describe?

Choices for question 6
Show the worked solution

Answer: D. Delta high-voltage winding, wye low-voltage winding with neutral, low voltage leading by 30 degrees.

The capital letter is the high-voltage connection, the small letter the low-voltage connection, n means the neutral is brought out, and the number is a clock position where each hour is 30 degrees. Eleven o'clock is 30 degrees leading.

Related: Glossary: Vector Group

Question 7

What is a K-rated transformer designed to do?

Choices for question 7
Show the worked solution

Answer: C. Carry loads rich in harmonic current without overheating.

Harmonic currents cause extra eddy-current heating in the windings. A K-rated transformer is built to carry a load of a stated K-factor at full nameplate current. It tolerates the harmonics; it does not filter them out.

Related: Glossary: K-Factor

Question 8

What does a transformer's BIL rating describe?

Choices for question 8
Show the worked solution

Answer: D. The peak impulse voltage its insulation can withstand, as from lightning or switching.

Basic insulation level is an impulse rating, established with a standard 1.2 by 50 microsecond wave and stated as a peak in kilovolts. It is much higher than the working voltage because it describes a brief surge, not steady operation.
