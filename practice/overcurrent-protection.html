Overcurrent Protection: Practice Questions

Continuous loads, the 125 percent rule, standard ratings, interrupting rating and SCCR.

The sizing questions use the standard ampere ratings: 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 100, 110, 125, 150, 175, 200 and upward. The rest test the difference between ratings that are often confused.

Question 1

A feeder supplies a continuous load of 52 A and nothing else. What is the smallest standard breaker that may be used?

Choices for question 1
Show the worked solution

Answer: B. 70 A

Minimum rating = 52 × 1.25 = 65 A. There is no 65 A standard rating, so the next one up applies: 70 A. A 60 A breaker allows only 48 A of continuous load.

Related: Breaker Size Calculator

Question 2

A feeder supplies 40 A of continuous load and 20 A of non-continuous load. What is the smallest standard breaker?

Choices for question 2
Show the worked solution

Answer: D. 70 A

Minimum rating = (40 × 1.25) + 20 = 70 A, which is itself a standard rating: 70 A. Only the continuous part takes the 125 percent factor.

Related: Breaker Size Calculator

Question 3

What is the largest continuous load a standard 20 A breaker may carry?

Choices for question 3
Show the worked solution

Answer: A. 16 A

A standard breaker may carry 80 percent of its rating continuously: 20 × 0.8 = 16 A. It may carry the full 20 A for a load that is not continuous.

Related: Breaker Size Chart

Question 4

A calculation gives a minimum rating of 112 A. What is the next standard ampere rating?

Choices for question 4
Show the worked solution

Answer: A. 125 A

The standard ratings around this value are 100, 110, 125 and 150 A. 110 A is below the minimum, and 115 and 120 A are not standard ratings, so the answer is 125 A.

Related: Breaker Size Chart

Question 5

What must a circuit breaker's interrupting rating be compared with?

Choices for question 5
Show the worked solution

Answer: D. The fault current available at the point where it is installed.

Interrupting rating is the largest fault current the device can clear safely, so it has to be at least the available fault current at its terminals. That current depends on the supply, mainly the size and impedance of the upstream transformer, and has nothing to do with the load.

Related: Glossary: Interrupting Rating

Question 6

How does a short-circuit current rating (SCCR) differ from an interrupting rating?

Choices for question 6
Show the worked solution

Answer: D. SCCR applies to a whole assembly; interrupting rating applies to a single protective device.

An assembly such as a control panel contains many components in the fault path, and its SCCR is set by the weakest of them unless a current-limiting device protects it. A panel with a 65 kA main breaker can still be a 5 kA panel.

Related: Interrupting Rating vs. SCCR

Question 7

In a selectively coordinated system, what happens when a fault occurs on a branch circuit?

Choices for question 7
Show the worked solution

Answer: A. Only the protective device nearest the fault opens.

Coordination means each upstream device is slower than the one below it across the range of fault currents, so the fault is cleared locally and everything else stays energized. It is checked by comparing time-current curves.

Related: Glossary: Selective Coordination

Question 8

A breaker and its enclosure are listed for operation at 100 percent of rating. For a continuous load of 400 A and no other load, what is the smallest standard breaker?

Choices for question 8
Show the worked solution

Answer: C. 400 A

For an assembly listed for 100 percent operation the continuous load is taken at 100 percent, so the minimum is 400 A and a 400 A breaker is sufficient. A standard breaker would need 400 × 1.25 = 500 A. The listing has to say 100 percent; it is not implied by frame size.
