FAT vs. SAT: Factory and Site Acceptance Testing Explained

A factory acceptance test proves the equipment was built right before it ships. A site acceptance test proves it arrived intact and was installed right. Each catches problems the other cannot, and both have to be written into the order to exist.

By Voltfield Editorial Team·Published November 15, 2026·How we source & verify this

Key takeaways

  • FAT happens at the manufacturer before shipment and checks the equipment against the specification and approved drawings.
  • SAT happens after installation and checks that nothing was damaged in transit and that the equipment works in its real surroundings.
  • A problem found at the factory is fixed by the people and tools that built the equipment. The same problem found on site costs many times more.
  • Witness and hold points must be in the purchase order. So must the rule that equipment does not ship until the punch list is closed or agreed.

The difference in one paragraph

A factory acceptance test (FAT) is carried out at the manufacturer’s works before the equipment ships, to show that it meets the specification. A site acceptance test (SAT) is carried out after the equipment is installed, to show that it survived the journey and works where it will be used. The FAT is the buyer’s last chance to have a fault corrected in the factory. The SAT is the first proof that the installed system does its job.

What a FAT covers

The content depends on the equipment, and the governing standard names the tests. Standards generally sort tests into routine tests, done on every unit, and design tests (also called type tests), done once on a representative unit to prove the design. A buyer normally receives routine test results for its own units and design test reports from an earlier identical or similar unit, unless the order pays for the design tests to be repeated.

  • Transformers: ratio, polarity and phase relation, winding resistance, no-load loss and excitation current, load loss and impedance, dielectric tests, and for liquid-filled units a leak test. The measured losses are the ones compared with the guaranteed figures from the bid evaluation.
  • Switchgear and switchboards: a check against the drawings, wiring and control checks, mechanical operation and interlocks, dielectric tests, and functional tests of protection and control with relays set and trip circuits proven.
  • Generators and UPS: load tests in steps up to rated load, transient response to load steps, alarms and shutdowns, and for a UPS the transfers to and from bypass and battery.
  • Packaged systems, such as power skids and battery containers: an integrated test with everything connected, including controls, communications and fire detection interfaces.

Alongside the electrical tests there is a visual and dimensional inspection: nameplate data, bill of materials, paint, labels, terminal markings, and the anchoring and cable entry details the site crew is counting on.

Witness points, hold points and the ITP

The document that organizes all this is the inspection and test plan (ITP). It lists each inspection and test in sequence, the procedure and acceptance criteria for it, and what the buyer’s involvement is. That involvement comes in three levels:

  • Hold point. Work stops until the buyer has attended or has released the point in writing.
  • Witness point. The buyer is notified and may attend. If the buyer does not come, the work goes on.
  • Review. The buyer receives the records afterwards.

Ask for the ITP with the approval drawings and agree it before fabrication. Put the notice period for witness points in the order: ten working days is common, since travel has to be booked. And decide who attends. A witness who does not know the equipment can only confirm that a test took place.

The punch list and release for shipment

Almost every FAT finds something: a mislabelled terminal, a relay setting, a missing accessory, sometimes a failed test. Each finding goes on a punch list with an owner and a date. The decision that matters is which items must be closed before shipment and which can be finished on site.

The rule of thumb is simple. Anything that needs factory tools, factory people or a retest stays at the factory. Agreeing to “fix it on site” moves the cost and the schedule risk to the project, and field repairs to a transformer or a tested assembly are rarely as good as factory ones. The order should say that the equipment is not released for shipment until the buyer signs the release, and should tie a payment milestone to that signature.

The FAT report, the test data and the closed punch list travel with the documents the order requires. Check them against the delivery when it arrives, using the proof of delivery.

What a SAT covers

Transport and installation are hard on equipment. A SAT starts with what the journey may have changed and then moves to what the factory could not test:

  • Receiving checks: impact recorder readings, pressure or oil level, visible damage.
  • Repeat of selected factory tests to compare with the FAT values: insulation resistance, turns ratio, winding resistance, contact resistance. A change from the factory figure is the signal to look for.
  • Installation checks: anchoring, clearances, grounding and bonding, cable terminations and torque, phasing.
  • Functional tests with the real system: protection trips to the actual breakers, interlocks between pieces of equipment from different vendors, controls and monitoring through to the plant system.
  • Energization and load, with readings recorded.

Site testing of electrical equipment is commonly specified to recognized acceptance testing standards, which give the tests and expected values for each equipment type. Whether the SAT is the same thing as commissioning or one step within it varies from project to project, so define it in the contract.

Common mistakes

  • Not putting the FAT in the order. A witness test requested after award is a change order.
  • Waiving the FAT to save schedule. The time saved is usually spent several times over on site.
  • Accepting design test reports from a unit that is not really similar.
  • Shipping with an open punch list and no agreement on who closes it.
  • Skipping the comparison between site and factory values. A site test result means most when set beside the factory one.
  • Treating a signed delivery receipt as acceptance. Acceptance comes after the SAT.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between FAT and SAT?

A factory acceptance test is done at the manufacturer before shipment to prove the equipment meets the specification. A site acceptance test is done after installation to prove it was not damaged in transit, was installed correctly and works with the real system.

Is a factory acceptance test mandatory?

Manufacturers perform routine tests on every unit as their standards require, but a FAT witnessed by the buyer happens only if the purchase order calls for it. For major equipment it is strongly advisable.

What is the difference between a hold point and a witness point?

At a hold point the manufacturer cannot proceed until the buyer has attended or released the point in writing. At a witness point the buyer is notified and may attend, but work continues if the buyer does not come.

What is an inspection and test plan?

A document that lists every inspection and test in order, with the procedure, the acceptance criteria and the buyer’s involvement at each step: hold, witness or review of records.
