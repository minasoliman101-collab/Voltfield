NEC 110.26: Working Space Around Electrical Equipment

The clearance rule that gets designed out on paper and discovered on site. Three dimensions, a condition that changes one of them, a dedicated space above the equipment, and an entrance requirement that applies once the gear gets large.

By Voltfield Editorial Team·Published November 3, 2026·How we source & verify this

Key takeaways

  • Three dimensions: depth in front of the equipment, 30 inches of width, and 6.5 feet of height. Width and height do not change with voltage.
  • Depth depends on what is on the opposite side, which the code calls Condition 1, 2 or 3. At 151 to 1000 volts those are 3, 3.5 and 4 feet.
  • The space above a panel is separately reserved. Piping and ducts not serving the equipment cannot pass through it.
  • Equipment rated 1200 A or more and over 6 feet wide needs an entrance at each end, unless an exception is satisfied.

The three dimensions

NEC 110.26(A) requires working space at any equipment likely to need examination, adjustment, servicing or maintenance while energized. It is specified in three directions and all three have to be satisfied at once.

Depth, under 110.26(A)(1), is measured from the exposed live parts or from the enclosure front where parts are enclosed, and it is the one that varies. Width, under 110.26(A)(2), is 30 inches or the width of the equipment, whichever is greater, and the space has to allow any door or hinged panel to open at least 90 degrees. Height, under 110.26(A)(3), is 6.5 feet or the height of the equipment, whichever is greater.

The width is often misread. It does not have to be centred on the equipment, but it does have to be continuous, and it is measured in front of the equipment rather than along it. Nothing may be stored in any of it — 110.26(B) is explicit that the working space is not storage space, which is the most common field violation on the whole section.

Depth and the three conditions

Depth changes with what a worker could contact behind them while reaching into energized equipment. The code names three cases:

  • Condition 1 — exposed live parts on one side, and nothing live or grounded on the other, or the other side effectively guarded by suitable insulating material.
  • Condition 2 — exposed live parts on one side and grounded parts on the other. Concrete, brick and tile walls count as grounded.
  • Condition 3 — exposed live parts on both sides of the working space, not guarded.

For nominal voltage to ground of 151 to 1000 volts those give 3 feet, 3 feet 6 inches and 4 feet respectively. At 0 to 150 volts all three conditions are 3 feet, which is why a residential panel is a straightforward 36 inches regardless of what is opposite it.

The practical trap is Condition 2. A panel on a wall facing a concrete wall three feet away looks compliant against the familiar 36-inch figure and is not, because the opposite wall is grounded and the requirement is 42 inches.

The dedicated equipment space

110.26(E) is a separate requirement and it is routinely confused with working space. Working space is in front of the equipment and is about access. Dedicated space is above and below it and is about keeping other trades out.

For indoor installations the space above the equipment footprint, extending from the floor to a height of 6 feet above the equipment or to the structural ceiling — whichever is lower — is reserved for electrical. Piping, ducts and equipment foreign to the electrical installation cannot be installed in it. Sprinkler protection is permitted, and a suspended ceiling with removable panels counts as the structural ceiling for this purpose.

This is the requirement that gets lost in coordination. The electrical layout is correct, the mechanical contractor runs a condensate line across the ceiling above a panelboard, and the violation is discovered at inspection with the pipe already hung.

Entrances to working space

110.26(C) governs getting out. Every working space needs at least one entrance of sufficient area, and large equipment needs more than one.

Where equipment is rated 1200 A or more and is over 6 feet wide, there must be an entrance at each end of the working space, not less than 24 inches wide and 6.5 feet high. Two exceptions allow a single entrance: where the location permits a continuous and unobstructed way of exit, or where the working space depth is twice the figure 110.26(A)(1) requires and the single entrance is positioned so a worker never has to pass within that minimum depth to leave.

Where an entrance serves such equipment, personnel doors less than 25 feet from the nearest edge of the working space must open in the direction of egress and be equipped with panic hardware or listed fire-exit hardware. That requirement exists because the thing you are escaping is an arc flash, and it is not a dimension anyone can negotiate.

Illumination and elevation

110.26(D) requires illumination for all working spaces about service equipment, switchboards, switchgear, panelboards and motor control centers installed indoors. It cannot be controlled by automatic means alone, which rules out a lone occupancy sensor as the only control, and in general a luminaire is required rather than merely borrowed light.

110.26(A)(4) covers limited access, such as equipment above a suspended ceiling or in a crawl space, with its own access and clearance rules. 110.27 and 110.28 then deal with guarding live parts and enclosure types, which are separate subjects frequently cited alongside this one.

Common mistakes

  • Using 36 inches everywhere. It is the Condition 1 figure. A grounded surface opposite makes it 42 inches above 150 volts.
  • Storing anything in the working space. 110.26(B) prohibits it outright, and a stack of boxes in front of a panel is a violation whether or not the panel is being worked on.
  • Confusing dedicated space with working space. One is in front and about access, the other is above and about other trades.
  • Measuring width along the equipment instead of in front of it. The 30 inches is depth-wise clear space in front, not a strip beside the gear.
  • Forgetting the 90-degree door swing. A panel in a narrow corridor can satisfy every dimension and still fail because the door cannot open.

Frequently asked questions

How much clearance does NEC 110.26 require in front of a panel?

Depth depends on the condition. For nominal voltage to ground of 151 to 1000 volts it is 3 feet for Condition 1, 3 feet 6 inches for Condition 2 and 4 feet for Condition 3. At 0 to 150 volts all three conditions are 3 feet. Width is 30 inches or the equipment width, whichever is greater, and height is 6.5 feet or the equipment height.

What is the difference between Condition 1, 2 and 3?

Condition 1 has exposed live parts on one side and nothing live or grounded opposite. Condition 2 has exposed live parts on one side and grounded parts opposite, which includes concrete, brick and tile walls. Condition 3 has exposed live parts on both sides of the space. Each step increases the required depth above 150 volts.

Can storage be placed in electrical working space?

No. NEC 110.26(B) requires the working space to be kept clear and states it is not to be used for storage. It applies all the time, not only while the equipment is being serviced, and it is the most commonly cited violation in the section.

What is dedicated equipment space?

A separate requirement in NEC 110.26(E) covering the space above and below the equipment footprint rather than in front of it. Indoors, the space above extends to 6 feet above the equipment or to the structural ceiling, whichever is lower, and piping, ducts and other equipment foreign to the electrical installation cannot be placed in it. Sprinkler protection is permitted.
