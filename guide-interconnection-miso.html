MISO Interconnection Timeline, Explained

One RTO stretching from Manitoba to the Gulf Coast, one nominal process, and wildly different timelines depending on where in that footprint a project actually sits.

By Voltfield Editorial Team·Published Aug 23, 2026·Updated August 24, 2026·How we source & verify this
Share this guide: LinkedIn X Email

This guide assumes the general interconnection process as background. MISO is the largest RTO by geographic footprint in North America — a north-south corridor running from Manitoba, Canada down through the upper Midwest into the lower Mississippi Valley — and FERC-jurisdictional throughout.

The Definitive Planning Phase (DPP)

MISO's cluster-study stage is called the Definitive Planning Phase, or DPP: a cohort of interconnection requests filed within the same window is studied together for system impacts and the specific network upgrades each project would require, following the same feasibility-study-then-system-impact-study logic covered in the general interconnection guide, just run for a batch rather than one request at a time. It's MISO's version of the cluster-study concept FERC Order 2023 later mandated industry-wide.

Why DPP cycles ran so far behind schedule

MISO's DPP process became a widely-cited example of interconnection queue strain: the volume of wind, solar, and storage interconnection requests grew far faster than MISO's study capacity could absorb in several cycles, and cohorts targeted to complete on a schedule measured in months instead stretched to multiple years. That experience — MISO's own version of the same industry-wide backlog problem — fed directly into both MISO-specific process reforms and the broader push toward FERC Order 2023's stricter readiness requirements and use-it-or-lose-it deadlines.

Why this matters for procurement: a DPP cohort running behind schedule doesn't just delay the interconnection agreement — it delays the facilities study that specifies exact equipment ratings, which pushes back the point at which a project can place a firm equipment order with confidence. Track DPP cycle status directly, not just an original filing-to-completion estimate.

Why MISO's footprint size matters more than most

MISO's north-south geographic span means transmission congestion and available capacity look completely different depending on sub-region. MISO South — added later, covering much of the lower Mississippi Valley and built around the transmission systems formerly operated independently there — has its own congestion patterns distinct from the upper Midwest. A project's specific location inside MISO's footprint, not just its MISO membership, is what actually determines which network upgrades a system impact study is likely to identify and how long they'll take to build.

How this compares to neighboring regions

MISO borders PJM to the east and SPP to the west, and a project near either boundary should confirm which RTO's queue it actually falls in — that determines everything from study timeline to which tariff governs readiness deposits. None of MISO's DPP-specific mechanics transfer to a neighboring region's process.

Frequently asked questions

What is MISO's Definitive Planning Phase?

The Definitive Planning Phase (DPP) is MISO's cluster-study stage, where a batch (cohort) of interconnection requests filed in the same cycle is studied together for system impacts and required network upgrades — MISO's implementation of the same cluster-study concept FERC Order 2023 later required industry-wide.

Why did MISO's DPP cycles run so far behind schedule?

The volume of interconnection requests, driven heavily by wind, solar, and storage development, grew far faster than MISO's study capacity in several DPP cycles, pushing cohorts that were targeted to complete in months out to multiple years — a pattern that prompted both MISO-specific reforms and, later, the industry-wide FERC Order 2023 changes.

Does MISO's interconnection timeline vary by region?

Yes. MISO's footprint runs from Manitoba to the Gulf Coast, including a South region added later covering much of the lower Mississippi Valley, and transmission congestion and required network upgrades differ significantly by sub-region — a project's specific location inside MISO's footprint matters more than its MISO membership alone.
