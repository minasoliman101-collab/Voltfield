NEC 240.87: Arc Energy Reduction

Incident energy is roughly proportional to how long the arc burns, so the code requires a deliberate way of clearing faster on large devices. One obvious-looking approach is specifically not an accepted method.

By Voltfield Editorial Team·Published November 3, 2026·How we source & verify this

Key takeaways

  • Incident energy scales with arcing time, so halving the clearing time roughly halves the energy a worker would be exposed to.
  • The requirement applies where the overcurrent device is rated or can be adjusted to 1200 A or more.
  • Temporarily lowering the instantaneous trip setting for maintenance is not an accepted method. Energy-reducing maintenance switching with a local status indicator is.
  • The system has to be performance tested when first installed, and the method and documentation location have to be recorded.

Why clearing time is the lever

Incident energy is what an arc flash study produces and what personal protective equipment is selected against, and it depends on three things: the arcing current, the working distance, and how long the arc burns.

Of those, the arcing current is set by the system and the working distance is set by the task. The duration is the one a designer can change, and it responds proportionally — cut the clearing time in half and the incident energy falls by roughly half. Nothing else available at the design stage moves the number that directly.

That is the whole basis of 240.87. On large devices, the settings that give good selective coordination in normal operation tend to be exactly the settings that let an arcing fault burn for a long time. The section requires a deliberate way out of that trade-off rather than leaving it to the coordination study.

Where the requirement applies

The trigger is the device rating, and the wording is broader than it first appears: it applies where the highest continuous current trip setting for which the installed overcurrent device is rated, or can be adjusted, is 1200 A or more.

The adjustable clause catches installations people assume are outside the scope. A breaker in a 2000 A frame set to 1000 A is still a device that can be adjusted to 1200 A or more, so it is in scope at the setting it could carry rather than the setting it currently has. The question is what the device is capable of, not what someone dialled in.

Fuses have their own parallel requirement at 240.67, on the same 1200 A basis, which is a more recent addition and still gets missed on fused services. A system is not outside the arc energy reduction requirements because it uses fuses rather than breakers.

The accepted methods

The section lists specific means, and any one of them satisfies it. They are not equivalent in cost or in what they protect.

Zone-selective interlocking lets a downstream device tell an upstream one that it has seen the fault, so the upstream device holds in only while that communication is present and otherwise trips without delay. It preserves coordination and reduces clearing time, which is why it is the common choice on new switchgear.

Differential relaying compares current in and out of a protected zone and trips on the difference, which is inherently fast and does not depend on coordination margins at all.

Energy-reducing maintenance switching with a local status indicator provides a separate, lower-set protection scheme that a worker enables before working on the equipment and that visibly indicates it is active. This is the retrofit answer and by far the most common on existing installations.

An energy-reducing active arc flash mitigation system detects the arc and extinguishes or diverts it, typically in a few milliseconds, which reduces energy without altering the protection settings.

Also listed: an instantaneous trip setting below the available arcing current, an instantaneous override, and an approved equivalent means. The first two are the cheapest where the coordination study allows them, and that is the condition that usually rules them out.

The method that does not count

There is an obvious-looking approach that is specifically not acceptable, and it is worth stating plainly because it is what people reach for.

Temporarily turning down the instantaneous trip setting on a breaker before maintenance, and turning it back up afterwards, does not satisfy the requirement. It looks functionally identical to energy-reducing maintenance switching and it is not. What it lacks is the separate, deliberate switching function and the local status indicator — a visible confirmation at the equipment that the reduced-energy mode is actually engaged.

The reasoning is about failure modes rather than physics. A setting someone adjusted depends on that person having adjusted it correctly, on nobody having changed it since, and on it being restored afterwards. None of that is verifiable at the equipment by the next worker. A maintenance switch with an indicator is, and the whole point of the provision is that the person about to open the enclosure can confirm the protection they are relying on is live.

Testing and documentation

Two requirements attach to whichever method is chosen, and both are frequently the gap found at commissioning rather than in design.

The arc energy reduction system has to be performance tested when first installed, by a qualified person, to demonstrate it actually works. A scheme that exists on the drawings and in the relay settings file but has never been proven to operate does not meet the section.

The method and the location of its documentation have to be recorded and available. In practice this means field marking at the equipment identifying what method is in use and where the supporting documentation lives, so that the next person working on it knows what protection exists and how to engage it. An arc energy reduction scheme nobody knows about protects nobody.

Common mistakes

Reading the 1200 A threshold against the current setting rather than the rating or the adjustable range, and concluding a large frame set low is out of scope. Using a temporary change to the instantaneous setting in place of energy-reducing maintenance switching. Omitting the local status indicator, which is part of the method rather than an accessory to it. Assuming a fused service is outside the arc energy reduction requirements, when 240.67 applies on the same basis. Specifying an instantaneous setting below the arcing current without checking that the coordination study tolerates it. Treating the scheme as complete at design, with no performance test and no field marking. Confusing arc energy reduction, which is a design measure, with the PPE requirements in NFPA 70E, which are separate and still apply.

Frequently asked questions

When does NEC 240.87 apply?

Where the highest continuous current trip setting for which the installed overcurrent device is rated, or can be adjusted, is 1200 A or more. The adjustable clause matters: a breaker in a large frame set to 1000 A is still capable of 1200 A or more and is therefore in scope. Fuses have a parallel requirement at 240.67 on the same 1200 A basis.

What methods satisfy the arc energy reduction requirement?

Zone-selective interlocking, differential relaying, energy-reducing maintenance switching with a local status indicator, an energy-reducing active arc flash mitigation system, an instantaneous trip set below the available arcing current, an instantaneous override, or an approved equivalent means. Any one is sufficient, but they differ widely in cost and in whether an existing coordination study will tolerate them.

Can I just lower the instantaneous trip setting before maintenance?

No. Temporarily adjusting the setting is not an accepted method. The accepted equivalent is energy-reducing maintenance switching with a local status indicator, and the indicator is the point: a worker about to open the enclosure can confirm the reduced-energy mode is actually engaged, which they cannot do with a setting someone adjusted earlier and may or may not have restored.

Does an arc energy reduction system have to be tested?

Yes. It has to be performance tested when first installed, by a qualified person, to demonstrate it operates as intended. The method in use and the location of its documentation also have to be recorded and available at the equipment, so the next person working on it knows what protection exists and how to engage it.
