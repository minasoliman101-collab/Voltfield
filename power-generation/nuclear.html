Nuclear Power Plants: Electrical Systems and Equipment Qualification

The turbine hall of a nuclear plant looks like any large steam plant. What sets it apart is the electrical system that must keep cooling running whatever happens, and the paperwork behind every part in it.

A nuclear plant uses the heat of fission to make steam, and from there it is a steam power plant: turbine, generator, step-up transformer, grid. The difference is that a reactor keeps producing decay heat after it is shut down, so the plant needs reliable electrical power for cooling even when it is generating none. Much of the electrical design, and nearly all of what is distinctive about procurement, follows from that requirement.

How it makes power

In a pressurized water reactor (PWR), water in the reactor circuit is kept under pressure so it does not boil, and passes its heat through steam generators to a separate circuit that drives the turbine. In a boiling water reactor (BWR), water boils in the reactor vessel and the steam goes directly to the turbine. Most operating reactors are one of the two.

Steam conditions are modest compared with a fossil plant, so thermal efficiency is lower, typically around a third.

Electrical output = thermal power × efficiency

A reactor rated 3,400 MW thermal at 33% produces about 1,120 MW electrical. The rest is rejected through the condenser, which is why these plants sit beside large bodies of water or cooling towers. What nuclear plants lack in efficiency they make up in running hours: capacity factors above 90% are normal for the United States fleet.

Numbers worth knowing

  • Class 1E is the safety classification of electrical equipment essential to emergency reactor shutdown, containment isolation, core cooling and heat removal.
  • Redundancy is by division. Each safety division has its own switchgear, diesel generator and battery, separated physically and electrically so that one failure cannot disable both.
  • Two offsite circuits plus the onsite diesel generators is the usual basis. Loss of all of them is a station blackout, which plants are required to be able to cope with for a defined time.
  • Capacity factor is typically above 90%, with refueling outages every 18 to 24 months as the main planned downtime.
  • Small modular reactors are designs of up to roughly 300 MW electrical per module, intended to be factory-built. Several rely more on passive cooling and so on less safety-related AC power.

Buying and shipping it

Safety-related equipment is bought under a nuclear quality assurance program: in the United States, 10 CFR Part 50 Appendix B, normally implemented through ASME NQA-1. Suppliers are audited and placed on an approved list before they can be used. The equipment itself must be qualified, with documented evidence that it will perform through its service life and under accident conditions (IEEE 323) and during and after an earthquake (IEEE 344). Defects must be reportable under 10 CFR Part 21. Where no qualified supplier exists, a commercial item can be accepted through commercial-grade dedication, which verifies its critical characteristics by test and inspection. All of this means the documentation is a deliverable as real as the hardware, traceability runs to the individual part, and lead times and prices are well beyond those of the commercial equivalent. Non-safety equipment on the same site is bought much like that of any large power plant.

Questions

What is Class 1E equipment?

The safety classification for electrical equipment and systems that are essential to emergency reactor shutdown, containment isolation, reactor core cooling and containment and reactor heat removal, or otherwise essential in preventing a significant release of radioactive material.

What is the difference between a PWR and a BWR?

In a pressurized water reactor the water in the reactor is kept under high pressure so it does not boil, and it heats a separate circuit through steam generators. In a boiling water reactor the water boils in the reactor vessel and that steam drives the turbine directly.

What is commercial-grade dedication?

A process for accepting an item that was not made under a nuclear quality assurance program for use in a safety-related application. The item’s critical characteristics are identified and verified by inspection, testing or supplier survey, after which it is treated as a basic component.
