How to Compare Equipment Bids: Bid Tabulation and Total Evaluated Cost

Three quotes for the same transformer rarely describe the same transformer. A bid tabulation puts them on one basis, and a loss evaluation shows what each will cost to own. Both fit on a page.

By Voltfield Editorial Team·Published November 15, 2026·How we source & verify this

Key takeaways

  • Bids can only be compared once they describe the same scope. Leveling the scope comes before comparing the price.
  • Read the exceptions and clarifications first. That is where a low price is usually explained.
  • For transformers, add the cost of losses: total owning cost = price + A × no-load loss + B × load loss.
  • Lead time, payment terms, warranty and documentation are part of the comparison, and should be written down beside the price.

What a bid tabulation is

A bid tabulation is a table with the bidders across the top and everything that matters down the side: price, scope, technical data, delivery and commercial terms. Its job is to put every bid on the same basis so that the differences between them are visible, and to leave a record of why one was chosen.

It only works if every bidder was asked the same question. That is the purpose of a proper request for quotation: one specification, one data sheet to fill in, one set of commercial terms, and one deadline. When bidders are given different information, the table compares the bidders’ guesses.

Step 1: level the scope

Before looking at totals, go line by line and mark what each bid includes. The usual gaps are:

  • Freight and unloading. One bid is delivered to site, another is priced at the factory. See the Incoterms guide.
  • Accessories and options. Monitoring, surge arresters, special paint, seismic bracing, spare parts.
  • Testing. Routine tests only, or the optional tests the specification asked for. Witness testing included or extra. See FAT vs. SAT.
  • Site services. Assembly, oil filling, commissioning support, training.
  • Taxes and duties.

Where a bid leaves something out, ask the bidder for a price, in writing, and enter it. If time does not allow, enter the highest price any other bidder gave for that item and mark it as an allowance. What you must not do is compare a complete bid with an incomplete one and call the difference a saving.

Step 2: read the exceptions

Every serious bid has a section called exceptions, clarifications or deviations. It lists where the offer departs from your specification and your terms. Typical entries: a different impedance, a lower temperature rating, a substitute component manufacturer, a shorter warranty, a cap on liability, a refusal of liquidated damages.

Each exception is one of three things. It is acceptable as it stands, it is acceptable at a price, or it disqualifies the bid. Decide which, with the engineer for technical items and whoever owns the contract terms for commercial ones, and record the decision in the table. A bid with no exceptions listed deserves a second look as well: either the bidder complies in full, or the bidder did not read the specification.

Step 3: add the cost of losses

A transformer consumes energy every hour it is energized. No-load loss is drawn by the core all the time, whatever the load. Load loss is drawn in the windings and rises with the square of the load. Over a service life of decades the cost of that energy can rival the purchase price, and two bids at a similar price can differ a great deal in losses.

The standard way to bring losses into the comparison is to give each kilowatt of loss a present value and add it to the price:

Total owning cost = purchase price + (A × no-load loss) + (B × load loss)

A and B are in dollars per kilowatt. The owner sets them from its energy cost, the expected loading, the years of service and its discount rate. A is always the larger, since no-load loss runs continuously. State both factors in the request for quotation, so bidders can design to them, and require guaranteed loss figures with a stated tolerance.

An example, with round illustrative numbers that are not market prices, and assumed factors of A = $8,000 per kW and B = $2,500 per kW:

Bidder 1Bidder 2
Purchase price$100,000$94,000
No-load loss2.5 kW3.4 kW
Load loss22 kW26 kW
A × no-load loss$20,000$27,200
B × load loss$55,000$65,000
Total owning cost$175,000$186,200

Bidder 2 is $6,000 cheaper to buy and $11,200 more expensive to own. The same reasoning applies, with different inputs, to the efficiency of a UPS, a motor or a chiller: wherever equipment runs for years, the energy it wastes belongs in the comparison.

Step 4: compare the terms that are not a price

Some differences cannot be turned into dollars cleanly, and they still decide projects. Put them in the table in plain words:

  • Lead time, stated from the same starting point for each bidder: from order, or from drawing approval. These can differ by months. Note how long the drawing cycle is and whether the date is guaranteed.
  • Payment terms. A large payment with the order costs money and removes leverage. Compare the milestones side by side.
  • Warranty. Length, when it starts, and whether it covers removal, shipping and reinstallation or only the repair.
  • Late delivery. Whether liquidated damages were accepted, and at what rate and cap.
  • Where it is built and tested, and by whom. This matters for domestic content rules and for witness testing.
  • Service and spares after delivery.
  • Validity. How long the price holds, and whether it is fixed or tied to a metals index.

Step 5: recommend, and write down why

The finished table should let someone who was not in the room see why the recommended bidder was chosen. State the basis in a sentence: lowest total owning cost among technically compliant bids that meet the required date, for example. If the recommendation is not the lowest evaluated cost, say what outweighed it. Keep the bids, the clarification emails and the table together. The bid tabulation template has a place for each of these.

Treat bidders evenly throughout. A clarification that changes the scope goes to all of them, and one bidder’s price is never shown to another.

Frequently asked questions

What is a bid tabulation?

A table that lists each bidder’s price, scope, technical data, delivery and commercial terms side by side on the same basis, so the bids can be compared fairly and the reason for the award is recorded.

What are A and B factors in a transformer bid evaluation?

They are dollar values per kilowatt that an owner assigns to transformer losses. A applies to no-load loss and B to load loss. Total owning cost is the purchase price plus A times the no-load loss plus B times the load loss, and bids are compared on that total.

Should I always pick the lowest bid?

Pick the lowest bid only after the scopes have been leveled, exceptions resolved and operating costs added. The lowest price on the cover page is often missing scope, carries exceptions to the specification or has higher losses.

How many bids do I need?

Three is the usual minimum for a meaningful comparison, and many organizations’ procurement rules require it. For long-lead equipment with few qualified manufacturers, fewer may be all that is available; record why.
