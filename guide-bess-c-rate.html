BESS C-Rate Explained

A 4-hour battery runs at 0.25C, but the rate its cells actually see is usually higher, and it rises further as the battery ages. Here's what C-rate means, how to calculate it on the right basis, and why it decides cell choice, cooling and warranty terms.

By Voltfield Editorial Team·Published September 29, 2026·How we source & verify this

Key takeaways

  • C-rate is how fast a battery charges or discharges relative to its capacity: a 100 MW / 400 MWh system is a 4-hour system at 0.25C.
  • Always state the basis: point of interconnection, DC, nameplate and usable figures can all be correct and all different.
  • As capacity fades at constant power, C-rate rises, so size on end-of-life capacity, not day one.

C-rate is how fast a battery charges or discharges relative to its capacity. At 1C, a full charge is delivered in one hour. At 0.5C it takes two hours, and at 0.25C four. For a single cell, C-rate is current divided by amp-hour capacity. For a storage system, it's normally stated as power divided by energy: MW ÷ MWh.

C-rate = Power ÷ Energy   ·   Duration (hours) = 1 ÷ C-rate

That makes C-rate the link between a project's two headline numbers. A "100 MW / 400 MWh" system is a 4-hour system, and 100 ÷ 400 = 0.25C.

Duration and C-rate

30 minutes2CFrequency response, fast ancillary services

2 hours0.5CPeak shaving, energy arbitrage
4 hours0.25CCapacity and resource adequacy, solar shifting
8 hours0.125CLong-duration shifting

The C-rate column is arithmetic. The "typical use" column is indicative: the market a project serves sets the duration it needs, and markets and their rules change.

The basis matters: nameplate, usable, and at the cells

A system's headline rating is usually measured at the point of interconnection (POI): power and energy delivered to the grid. The cells see something different, for three reasons:

  • Conversion losses. Energy passes through the PCS, transformers and cabling on its way to the POI, so the cells must deliver more power than the POI receives.
  • Depth of discharge. The battery management system reserves part of the nameplate capacity, so only the usable window does the work.
  • Degradation. Capacity fades with age and cycling. At the same power, a smaller battery runs at a higher C-rate.

Worked example

A 50 MW / 200 MWh (4-hour) project at the POI, with an assumed 96% one-way efficiency from battery to POI and 90% depth of discharge:

  • Power at the battery: 50 ÷ 0.96 = 52.1 MW
  • Energy the battery must deliver: 200 ÷ 0.96 = 208.3 MWh of usable capacity
  • Nameplate capacity: 208.3 ÷ 0.90 = 231.5 MWh
  • C-rate on the headline rating: 50 ÷ 200 = 0.25C
  • C-rate on nameplate: 52.1 ÷ 231.5 = 0.225C
  • C-rate on usable capacity: 52.1 ÷ 208.3 = 0.25C

Now let the battery fade to 80% of its original nameplate, with no augmentation. Nameplate becomes 185.2 MWh and usable becomes 166.7 MWh. At the same 52.1 MW, the C-rate on usable capacity rises to 0.31C, and the system delivers full power for only 166.7 ÷ 52.1 = 3.2 hours. It's no longer a 4-hour asset. This is the gap augmentation is planned to close.

Which number to ask for: when comparing vendor offers, ask what basis each C-rate is quoted on: POI, DC, nameplate or usable. The same system can honestly be described as 0.225C or 0.25C, and the difference shows up in warranty terms.

The 96% efficiency and 90% depth of discharge are assumptions for the example. Use the vendor's figures for the actual PCS, transformer and battery. The one-way efficiency here is not the same as the round-trip efficiency used in the BESS Sizing Calculator, which covers the full charge-and-discharge cycle.

Why C-rate drives the design

Cell selection

Cells are designed for a range of C-rates. Energy-oriented cells, common in multi-hour utility storage, trade power capability for energy density and cycle life. Power-oriented cells do the reverse. Choosing a cell outside its design range either wastes money or shortens life.

Heat

Resistive heating inside a cell scales with the square of current. At the same capacity, doubling the C-rate roughly quadruples resistive heat generation, so the thermal management system is sized around it. A 1-hour system needs much more cooling per MWh than a 4-hour system built from similar cells.

Degradation and warranty

Higher C-rates generally accelerate capacity fade, alongside temperature and depth of discharge. Vendors write warranties around them: a maximum continuous C-rate, a limit on cycles or energy throughput, and an operating temperature range. The warranty assumes a C-rate, so a change in how the project is operated can change what the warranty covers.

Power conversion

Duration changes the ratio of PCS capacity to battery capacity. A 1-hour system needs four times the PCS rating per MWh of a 4-hour system. That's why two systems can share a cell and still be very different products.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting C-rate without its basis. POI, DC, nameplate and usable figures can all be correct and all different.
  • Sizing on day-one capacity. As capacity fades, C-rate at constant power rises, and duration falls.
  • Treating duration as a pricing detail. It changes the cells, the cooling, the PCS and the warranty.
  • Mixing one-way and round-trip efficiency. They answer different questions, and using one for the other mis-sizes the system.

Frequently asked questions

What is C-rate in a battery?

C-rate is how fast a battery charges or discharges relative to its capacity. At 1C a full charge is delivered in one hour, at 0.5C in two hours, and at 0.25C in four. For a storage system it's usually calculated as power divided by energy.

What C-rate is a 4-hour battery?

A 4-hour battery is 0.25C on its headline rating, since 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25. The rate its cells actually see depends on conversion losses, depth of discharge and degradation, and is often a little different.

Is a higher C-rate worse for a battery?

Higher C-rates generally generate more heat and accelerate capacity fade, which is why warranties set a maximum continuous C-rate. Cells designed for high power tolerate it better than energy-oriented cells, but the tradeoff is usually lower energy density or higher cost.

How do I calculate C-rate from MW and MWh?

Divide power by energy: C-rate = MW ÷ MWh. A 50 MW / 200 MWh system is 50 ÷ 200 = 0.25C. Say which basis both numbers are on (point of interconnection, DC, nameplate or usable), because the answer changes with it.
