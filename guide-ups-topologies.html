UPS Topologies: Double Conversion vs. Eco Mode

Every UPS trades protection against efficiency. Double conversion isolates the load from the utility at the cost of a few percent in losses; eco mode recovers most of them by letting utility power through. Here is how the main topologies differ, and how to read the IEC 62040-3 class on a datasheet.

By Voltfield Editorial Team·Published September 29, 2026·How we source & verify this

Key takeaways

  • IEC 62040-3 classifies UPS systems as VFI (double conversion), VI (line-interactive) or VFD (standby).
  • Eco mode recovers most of double conversion's losses by passing utility power through, so check harmonics and transfer tolerance before enabling it.
  • Read the full code, such as VFI-SS-111, and compare efficiency at the light loads data center UPS systems actually run at.

A UPS topology describes where the load gets its power from in normal operation, and what happens when the utility misbehaves. IEC 62040-3 classifies UPS systems by how dependent their output is on the input, in three classes:

ClassTopologyWhat the output depends on
VFIDouble conversion (online)Independent of input voltage and frequency
VILine-interactiveIndependent of input voltage within limits, but follows input frequency
VFDStandby (offline)Follows input voltage and frequency until it transfers

On a datasheet the class is followed by performance codes, for example VFI-SS-111. The letters after the class describe the output waveform in normal and battery operation, and the digits describe dynamic performance during transfers and load steps. Class 1 is the tightest.

Double conversion (VFI)

A rectifier converts incoming AC to DC, the DC bus charges the battery, and an inverter rebuilds clean AC for the load, continuously. Because the load always runs from the inverter, a utility outage causes no transfer at all: the battery simply takes over the DC bus. The load is isolated from sags, surges, frequency drift and utility harmonics.

The cost is that every watt passes through two conversions. Modern large three-phase double-conversion units commonly quote efficiencies in the mid-90s percent at typical load, falling at light load, and that loss becomes heat the cooling system has to remove.

Eco mode (high-efficiency mode)

In eco mode, a double-conversion UPS runs the load on its static bypass, straight from the utility, with the inverter on standby. When the input goes out of tolerance, it transfers to the inverter within a few milliseconds. Efficiency typically rises to around 99%, because almost nothing is being converted.

The trade-off is exposure. While on bypass, the load sees utility disturbances until the transfer happens, and the UPS no longer filters the load's harmonics from the upstream system. Some manufacturers offer advanced modes that keep the inverter running in parallel to correct harmonics and power factor, which recovers part of the protection for part of the efficiency gain.

What the difference is worth

For 1 MW of IT load, a UPS at 96% efficiency loses 1,000 ÷ 0.96 − 1,000 = 41.7 kW. At 99% it loses 10.1 kW. The difference, 31.6 kW, is about 277 MWh a year, before the cooling energy saved by not having to remove it. That shows up directly in PUE. The efficiencies here are illustrative: use the manufacturer’s curve at the load you will actually run.

Line-interactive (VI)

The inverter sits in parallel with the utility path, and an automatic voltage regulator, usually a tapped transformer, corrects sags and swells without going to battery. On an outage the inverter takes over. Line-interactive units are efficient and common in smaller sizes, in IT closets and edge sites, but the output frequency follows the input, and there is a brief transfer on an outage.

Standby / offline (VFD)

The load runs directly from the utility, and the UPS switches to battery and inverter when the input fails. It is the simplest and cheapest topology, used for desktop and small loads. Transfer takes a few milliseconds.

Will the load ride through a transfer?

Server power supplies store enough energy to ride through a short interruption, and the ITIC (CBEMA) curve, the usual reference for IT equipment tolerance, allows a complete loss of voltage for up to 20 ms. A transfer of a few milliseconds is normally within that. Loads that are not IT power supplies, such as motors, drives and some controls, may be less tolerant and need checking.

Rotary and flywheel UPS

Not every UPS is static. Flywheel systems store energy mechanically and deliver seconds of ride-through, enough to start a generator but not much more. Diesel rotary UPS (DRUPS) couples a motor-generator and flywheel to a diesel engine in one machine. Both avoid large battery installations, at the price of mechanical maintenance. The backup power reference covers them alongside static UPS.

Modular or monolithic

Large UPS systems come either as single large units or as frames of hot-swappable power modules. Modular designs let capacity be added as load grows, keep modules closer to their efficient loading range, and put N+1 redundancy inside one frame. But the frame, its bypass and its controls are still shared, which the redundancy guide explains.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing efficiencies at full load. Data center UPS systems run lightly loaded, especially in 2N designs.
  • Enabling eco mode without checking the load. Harmonics and transfer tolerance need checking first.
  • Reading the class and ignoring the codes. VFI-SS-111 and a looser VFI rating are not the same product.
  • Forgetting the input side. Rectifier input current, harmonics and generator compatibility are part of the choice.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between VFI, VI and VFD UPS?

They are the IEC 62040-3 classes. VFI (double conversion) output is independent of input voltage and frequency; VI (line-interactive) output regulates voltage but follows input frequency; VFD (standby) output follows the input until the UPS transfers to battery.

What is UPS eco mode?

A mode in which a double-conversion UPS runs the load on its static bypass from the utility, with the inverter on standby, transferring to the inverter within a few milliseconds when the input goes out of tolerance. It raises efficiency to around 99% but exposes the load to utility disturbances until the transfer.

Is double conversion always better?

It gives the most protection, isolating the load from utility voltage and frequency disturbances with no transfer on an outage, but it costs a few percent in losses. Whether that is worth it depends on the load, the utility and the value of the energy.

Can servers ride through a UPS transfer?

Usually. Server power supplies store energy for short interruptions, and the ITIC curve allows a complete loss of voltage for up to 20 ms, longer than a typical transfer. Motors, drives and some controls may be less tolerant.
